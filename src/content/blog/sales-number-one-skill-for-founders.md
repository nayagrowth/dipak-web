## Startup

I think the word “startup” has become unnecessarily glamorous.

People hear startup and immediately imagine:

Funding.

Investors.

Pitch decks.

A cool office.

A founder with a MacBook sitting in a coffee shop. ☕😅

But a startup is actually much simpler than that.

A startup is a business trying to find a repeatable and scalable way to solve a real problem.

You're basically trying to answer four questions:

Does this problem actually exist?

Do people care enough about it to pay for a solution?

Can we solve it profitably?

Can we repeat this without everything depending on the founder?

And that last question is where many startups quietly start falling apart.


### When I was in business consulting...

I remember reading somewhere, possibly a Harvard-related source, that around 95% of startups in India failed within their first five years.

Later, I remember seeing the figure being revised closer to 90%.

I wouldn't get obsessed with whether the number is exactly 90% or 95%.

The bigger point is this:

A huge number of startups don't make it.

And whenever I think about why...

I don't immediately think about funding.

I think about something much more uncomfortable.

Did they actually understand the market?

And...

Did the founder know how to sell?

Because you can have a brilliant idea.

You can have a beautiful product.

You can have a great team.

And still build a company nobody wants to buy from.

That's a painful lesson.

But it's a very expensive one if you learn it after spending two years building the company.


## Here's something I think founders don't talk about enough.

Building a product can give you the illusion that you're making progress.

You can spend eight hours improving the product.

Nobody says no to you.

Nobody rejects you.

Nobody says:

> “I don't need this.”

You go home feeling productive.

Sales is different.

You call someone.

They say no.

You call another person.

They say, “Send me details.”

You follow up.

They disappear.

Someone says:

> “Your competitor is cheaper.”

Someone else says:

> “We'll think about it.”

And suddenly...

you have to confront reality.

😅

That's one reason I think some founders accidentally hide behind their product.

They aren't necessarily lazy.

They're doing work.

But they're doing work that doesn't force the market to answer the uncomfortable question:

> “Will anyone actually pay for this?”


### That's why I believe research comes before scaling.

A founder can fall in love with an idea very quickly.

> “I think people will love this.”

> “My friends think it's amazing.”

> “My team thinks it's brilliant.”

Great.

But here's the problem:

Your friends aren't the market.

And your customers don't owe you validation.

You can build something technically brilliant that solves a problem nobody considers urgent.

You can solve a real problem that people aren't willing to pay enough to solve.

You can build exactly what customers say they want...

and still discover that they don't actually buy it.

That's why I don't believe in asking only:

> “Do you like this idea?”

Ask:

> “Would you pay for it?”

Even better...

> “Have you already spent money trying to solve this problem?”

Now you're getting somewhere.

Because there's a huge difference between a problem someone complains about...

and a problem someone is willing to pay to remove.

That's one of those things nobody tells you when you're excited about starting a company.


### Here are some of the problems I see in startups:

Building before validating

Solving a problem nobody urgently wants solved

Talking to the wrong customers

Weak positioning

Poor pricing

Running out of cash

Hiring too early

Hiring the wrong people

Founder trying to do everything

No repeatable sales process

Depending too heavily on referrals

Building a product instead of building demand

Founder avoiding sales

Scaling before the business model is ready

And there's one problem hiding underneath many of these:

The founder hasn't figured out how to sell the thing yet.


## This is why I strongly believe sales is the #1 skill a startup founder should learn.

Not because the founder should become the salesperson forever.

Quite the opposite.

The founder needs to understand sales deeply enough to eventually build a sales machine that doesn't depend on the founder.

Think about it.

Who is the founder selling to?

Customers.

Investors.

Employees.

Co-founders.

Partners.

Suppliers.

Early hires.

Sometimes even the family members asking:

“Beta, exactly what are you doing?” 😅

Everyone has to believe in something.

A founder is constantly selling.

Not always for money.

Sometimes you're selling a vision.

Sometimes you're selling confidence.

Sometimes you're selling an opportunity.

Sometimes you're selling the idea that the company is worth joining.

And sometimes...

you're selling yourself the reason to keep going.

So if you can't convince people...

how exactly are you planning to build a company?


### And here's where I would tell every founder to read Sell Like Crazy.

Not because reading one sales book will magically make you a great salesperson.

It won't.

I recommend it because it made me think differently about where your time should actually go.

As a founder, everything looks important.

Product.

Marketing.

Finance.

Hiring.

Operations.

Technology.

Customer support.

Sales.

Your inbox has 47 things screaming:

“DO ME FIRST.” 😅

But the founder's job isn't to personally do all 47.

The founder's job is to figure out:

What only I can do?

What should I learn?

What should I delegate?

What should eventually become a system?

And sales is one of those things I don't think a founder should delegate too early.


## Here's my rule:

Before you hire your first salesperson, learn how to sell the product yourself.

Why?

Because you need to discover the sales process before you can delegate the sales process.

You need to know:

Who buys?

Why do they buy?

Why don't they buy?

What objections keep coming up?

What words do customers actually use?

What part of your offer excites them?

What part scares them?

What makes them finally say yes?

Once you know that...

now you have something you can teach.

Otherwise, what are you going to tell your first salesperson?

> “Here's the product. Here's your target. Go sell.”

😅

That's not delegation.

That's abandonment with a salary.


### And here's another thing nobody tells founders.

Your first salesperson is often not there to discover your sales process.

Your founder should have already discovered a big part of it.

The salesperson's job should be to execute and improve the process, not spend six months figuring out whether your customers even understand why they should buy.

That's a completely different starting point.


### Then comes the next challenge.

You finally have a salesperson who can sell.

Great.

But what happens if that salesperson leaves?

If tomorrow your best salesperson resigns...

and your revenue falls off a cliff...

you don't have a sales machine.

You have a sales dependency.

That's why building a strong sales team is not just about hiring talented closers.

You need:

A clear target market.

A clear offer.

A clear sales process.

Qualification.

Follow-up.

Objection handling.

Training.

Coaching.

Measurement.

And a system that allows someone else to reproduce what your best salesperson does.

That's when sales becomes scalable.


### This is where I see founders make another interesting mistake.

They blame the salesperson.

> “She's not closing.”

> “He isn't aggressive enough.”

> “They don't follow up properly.”

Maybe.

But maybe the salesperson isn't the real problem.

Maybe the leads are wrong.

Maybe the offer is weak.

Maybe the positioning is unclear.

Maybe the price doesn't make sense.

Maybe the founder hasn't given the salesperson enough proof.

Maybe the sales process is broken.

Maybe...

the market simply doesn't want what you've built.

And if the founder doesn't understand sales, every one of these problems looks like:

> “My salesperson is bad.”

That's dangerous.

Because you can fire five salespeople...

and keep the real problem.


## And that's why I don't think founders should be afraid of sales.

I've heard:

> “I'm not a salesperson.”

Okay.

But you're a founder.

You're going to have to convince someone of something.

Eventually.

You don't need to become loud.

You don't need to become aggressive.

You don't need to turn into the person who talks for 20 minutes without taking a breath. 😅

You need to learn how to:

Understand people.

Ask better questions.

Find real problems.

Communicate value.

Handle uncertainty.

Build trust.

Ask for decisions.

That's sales.

And those skills don't just help you acquire customers.

They help you build the company.


### So if you're building a startup, here's what I'd focus on.

Don't start by asking:

> “How do I scale?”

Ask:

> “Have I found a problem people will actually pay me to solve?”

Then:

> “Can I sell the solution myself?”

Then:

> “Can I make that sales process repeatable?”

Then:

> “Can I teach someone else to do it?”

And only then:

> “How aggressively can I scale this?”

Because scaling a broken process doesn't make the problem smaller.

It makes the problem bigger.


### A startup is basically one giant sales conversation.

You convince someone to invest.

You convince someone to join.

You convince someone to buy.

You convince someone to partner.

You convince someone to trust you.

And eventually...

you need to convince the market that the problem you're solving is important enough to pay you for.

So yes...

Build the product.

Build the technology.

Build the team.

Build the company.

But before you try to build a billion-dollar startup...

make sure you can answer one very simple question:

> “Can I get someone to pay me?”

Because before you can scale a business...

you first have to sell one.
