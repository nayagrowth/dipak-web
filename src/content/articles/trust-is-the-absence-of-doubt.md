There is a comfortable myth in sales that trust is a matter of personality. Be warm, be confident, build rapport, and trust will follow.

It does not hold up. Plenty of likeable salespeople lose deals to people the buyer found less charming but more credible. Rapport makes a conversation pleasant. It does not make a decision safe.

A more useful definition: **trust is the absence of doubt.** Not the presence of warmth — the absence of unresolved concern. Which means trust is not a personality trait you either have or lack. It is a state you can systematically produce.

## Doubt is cumulative and mostly invisible

Every conversation adds or removes doubt, usually in ways neither party names out loud.

Doubt accumulates when:

- A claim is made that the buyer cannot verify.
- A question is answered slightly beside the point.
- Enthusiasm outpaces evidence.
- A weakness is obviously being avoided.

Doubt reduces when:

- A limitation is named before the buyer finds it.
- A question is answered precisely, including "I don't know, I'll confirm."
- The buyer's situation is described back to them more clearly than they described it.
- The recommendation is sometimes *not* to buy.

Notice that most of the trust-building actions cost you something in the short term. That is exactly why they work. A concession against your own interest is expensive to fake, so it carries information.

## The three doubts worth addressing directly

Most stalled deals contain one of three unresolved doubts. Each has a different remedy.

**1. "I'm not sure this works for a situation like mine."**
Generic proof does not fix specific doubt. The remedy is precision: describe their constraints accurately enough that they conclude you understand the case, then be explicit about where the approach does and does not apply.

**2. "I'm not sure I can trust what I'm being told."**
This is a credibility doubt, and it is almost always made worse by more enthusiasm. The remedy is calibration — state confidence proportional to evidence. Overclaiming on a small point costs you belief on the large ones.

**3. "I'm not sure about the consequences for me."**
Someone has to sponsor this internally. If it goes badly, they carry it. The remedy is to make the downside explicit and survivable: what the first thirty days look like, what the early signals are, what happens if it underdelivers.

## Clarity is the mechanism, not charisma

Clarity does the work that charisma is usually credited for. When a buyer can articulate what they are getting, why it fits, what it costs them, and what happens if it does not work, there is very little doubt left to sit in.

That is the entire practice. Find the doubts, address them honestly, and stop adding new ones. What remains is trust — and it is far more durable than the kind built on being liked, because it does not depend on you being in the room.

## A practical test

At the end of your next conversation, ask yourself one question: *could the buyer now explain this accurately to a sceptical colleague, without me there?*

If yes, you have transferred certainty. If no, you have had a pleasant conversation, and the doubt is still sitting where it was.
