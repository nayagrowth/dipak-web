I've probably heard more sales advice than most salespeople should ever hear. 😅

And the strange thing is...

a lot of it works.

Until you put it in the wrong situation.

That's where things get interesting.

Because somewhere along the way, sales training started turning useful techniques into universal laws.

> “Always do this.”

> “Never say that.”

> “Always ask open-ended questions.”

> “Never discuss price first.”

> “Always build rapport before talking business.”

> “Always overcome objections.”

> “Never let the customer leave without closing.”

And after hearing enough of these...

you start believing there's a secret operating system for selling.

Learn the framework.

Memorize the script.

Apply it.

Close the deal.

Simple.

Except...

real buyers didn't get the memo. 😅


## I learned this while working in sales.

I don't remember the exact date, but it was during one of the phases of my career when I was working closely with a sales team.

I remember our manager conducting training for the team.

The environment was pretty typical.

A group of salespeople sitting together, notebooks open, everyone listening.

Our manager was explaining a particular sales framework.

The idea was simple:

When the customer says X, do Y.

It sounded logical.

Actually...

it sounded very convincing.

The framework had steps.

Questions.

Responses.

A sequence.

And the way it was being explained, it almost sounded like:

> “If you follow this properly, the customer will move forward.”

And I remember thinking:

Okay. Let's try it.

So the team started using it.

And that's when I noticed something strange.

It worked beautifully...

in some conversations.

But in others?

It felt completely unnatural.

Sometimes the customer didn't respond the way the framework expected.

Sometimes the buyer asked something completely different.

Sometimes the industry itself had a different buying process.

Sometimes the decision-maker wasn't even in the conversation.

And sometimes...

the framework was technically being followed, but the conversation was getting worse.

I remember feeling frustrated.

Then irritated.

Because I kept thinking:

> “If this is supposed to work, why isn't it working here?”

And then another thought came:

> “Maybe the problem isn't the salesperson.”

Maybe...

the framework was being treated like a law when it was actually just a tool.

That distinction changed the way I looked at sales training.


## And I don't blame my manager.

That's important.

I don't think he was deliberately teaching the team something wrong.

He was teaching what he knew.

The bigger problem was something I've seen in sales training again and again:


#### People sometimes confuse what worked for them with what works everywhere.

If you've sold one type of product...

to one type of buyer...

in one type of industry...

with one kind of sales cycle...

you can easily start believing:

> “This is how sales works.”

But maybe...

that's just how your sales environment worked.

And there's a huge difference.


## Think about this.

Would you use exactly the same sales conversation for:

A ₹2,000 consumer product?

A ₹2 lakh service?

A ₹20 lakh enterprise solution?

A ₹50 lakh industrial machine?

A consulting engagement?

A real-estate purchase?

A software subscription?

A business acquisition?

Of course not.

Because the buyer changes.

The risk changes.

The decision-making changes.

The sales cycle changes.

The number of decision-makers changes.

The consequences of making the wrong decision change.

So why would the exact same technique work identically in all of them?

It shouldn't.

And yet...

that's exactly how some sales training is taught.


## This is where I want to break one of the biggest sales beliefs.


### A framework is not a law.

A framework is a way of thinking.

A structure.

A map.

A tool.

It can be incredibly useful.

But it doesn't replace judgment.

Think about GPS.

You enter the destination.

It gives you a route.

Perfect.

But imagine the road is blocked.

The GPS says:

> “Turn left.”

You look ahead.

There's a huge construction barrier.

Do you say:

“But the framework says left.” 😅

No.

You adapt.

You find another route.

The map is useful.

But you still need someone in the driver's seat.

That's how I see sales frameworks.


#### Framework = map.


#### Judgment = knowing when the map doesn't match the road.

And real sales conversations are full of roads the map didn't predict.


## This is also why I get uncomfortable when someone says:

> “This technique works every time.”

Every time?

For whom?

In which industry?

At what price?

With what buyer?

At what stage?

With what level of trust?

Under what circumstances?

The moment someone says:

> “Always...”

or

> “Never...”

my next question is:

> “In what situation?”

Because sales is contextual.

And context changes everything.


## Here's another belief I want to challenge.


### “The more you talk, the more you sell.”

It sounds logical.

You're explaining the product.

You're answering questions.

You're presenting benefits.

You're handling objections.

You're giving examples.

You're proving value.

You're doing everything a salesperson is supposed to do.

Except...

sometimes you're doing too much.

I've made this mistake myself.

Especially when I was starting out.

A buyer would become quiet.

And instead of becoming curious...

I'd become nervous.

I'd think:

> “Why aren't they responding?”

So I'd explain more.

Another benefit.

Another example.

Another proof point.

Another reason.

And internally, I felt productive.

But sometimes...

I wasn't helping the buyer.

I was calming myself down.

That's a very uncomfortable realization.

Sometimes salespeople keep talking because silence makes them uncomfortable.

Not because the buyer needs more information.


## Think about what happens when someone says:

> “Hmm... I need to think about this.”

The old-school salesperson often thinks:

> “Objection! Handle it.”

So they immediately jump in.

> “Absolutely, but let me explain one more thing...”

Then another thing.

Then another.

The buyer is trying to process the decision.

The salesperson is trying to stop the buyer from processing the decision. 😅

Sometimes the best response is simply:

> “Of course. What part would you like to think about?”

Now you're learning.

You're not fighting.


## And this brings me to another sales belief:


### “Always overcome objections.”

I don't agree.

Some objections should be explored.

Some should be answered.

Some should be challenged.

Some aren't objections at all.

And some...

should simply be respected.

Imagine a buyer says:

> “The timing isn't right. We're going through a major internal change.”

Do you immediately launch your objection-handling framework?

Maybe.

Or maybe...

you say:

> “That makes sense. What would need to change internally before this becomes a priority?”

Now you're having a conversation.

Not performing a technique.

That's a major difference.


## Here's another one.


### “Always ask open-ended questions.”

It's good advice.

Sometimes.

But not always.

Imagine you ask:

> “Tell me more about your sales process.”

That's open-ended.

Good question.

But sometimes a more specific question gives you much better information:

> “How many people are currently responsible for following up with leads?”

Or:

> “How long does it usually take your team to contact a new lead?”

Or:

> “Who actually makes the final decision?”

The question isn't powerful because it's open-ended.

It's powerful because it reveals something useful.

That's a very different way of thinking.


## Same thing with “always build rapport first.”

I've seen salespeople spend ten minutes discussing:

Cricket.

Travel.

Weather.

Family.

The city.

Weekend plans.

Because somebody told them:

> “Rapport must come before business.”

😅

But what if the buyer has already blocked 30 minutes and wants to get straight to the problem?

Now your “rapport” is becoming friction.

The buyer might be thinking:

> “Can we just get to the point?”

Rapport is useful.

But forced rapport is not rapport.

Context decides.


## Same with price.

> “Never discuss price too early.”

Good principle in some situations.

Bad rule in others.

If someone has a budget of ₹2 lakh...

and you're selling a ₹25 lakh solution...

why waste an hour pretending price doesn't matter?

Sometimes bringing the investment into the conversation early actually saves everyone's time.

Again...

I'm not saying:

> “Always discuss price early.”

I'm saying:

> “Stop looking for absolute rules where context matters.”


## And this is where I think sales trainers can unintentionally create weak salespeople.

Not because frameworks are bad.

Because framework dependency is bad.

A salesperson learns:

> “If the buyer says this, I say that.”

So they memorize.

Buyer says something unexpected.

Salesperson freezes.

Why?

Because the situation wasn't in the script.

That's the problem.

The salesperson learned responses.

They didn't learn judgment.


## Think about two salespeople.


#### Salesperson A:

Has memorized 30 objection-handling responses.

Buyer gives objection #31.

Silence.


#### Salesperson B:

Has fewer scripts.

But understands:

buyer psychology

business context

decision-making

value

risk

urgency

questions

human behavior

Buyer gives objection #31.

Salesperson B thinks:

> “Interesting. What's actually happening here?”

That's the person I want.

Because sales doesn't happen inside a training manual.

It happens in reality.

And reality is wonderfully bad at following scripts. 😅


## Now, before someone misunderstands me...

I absolutely believe in sales frameworks.

In fact, I use them.

Some frameworks are extremely powerful.

Some principles work across industries.

Some questioning structures work almost everywhere.

Some psychological principles are deeply rooted in human behavior.

But here's my belief:

The truly universal sales principles are rare. Very rare.

And the reason they survive across industries is usually because they are based on human behavior, not a particular sales environment.

For example:

Understand before prescribing.

Don't lie.

Don't promise what you can't deliver.

Ask questions that improve your understanding.

Create value before asking for commitment.

Don't confuse pressure with persuasion.

These aren't tricks.

They're principles.

And principles are much more portable than techniques.


## That's the distinction I wish more salespeople understood.


#### Technique asks:

> “What should I say?”


#### Framework asks:

> “What should I look for?”


#### Judgment asks:

> “What's actually happening here?”

And that third question...

is where the real game begins.


## Because sometimes the best sales move is to talk.

Sometimes it's to ask.

Sometimes it's to challenge.

Sometimes it's to explain.

Sometimes it's to stay silent.

Sometimes it's to follow up tomorrow.

Sometimes it's to walk away.

Sometimes it's to say:

> “I don't think we're the right fit.”

And sometimes...

it's to say absolutely nothing.

The salesperson who knows only techniques has a problem:

They need the situation to look familiar.

The salesperson with judgment can handle unfamiliar situations.

That's the difference.


## And here's why the best salespeople sometimes talk less.

It's not because they have discovered some magical “talk less, close more” technique.

It's because they don't panic when they don't know what to say.

They observe.

They listen.

They diagnose.

They think.

Then they speak.

And because they have actually understood what's happening...

they often need fewer words.

A beginner thinks:

> “What should I say next?”

An experienced salesperson thinks:

> “What is actually happening right now?”

That's a completely different mental state.


## I remember seeing the opposite too.

A salesperson would finish asking a question...

the buyer would pause for three seconds...

and the salesperson would immediately jump in.

Three seconds.

That's all.

😅

They couldn't tolerate the silence.

So they started talking.

And sometimes that extra sentence...

created the very doubt that didn't exist before.

That's one of the strangest things about selling.

You can talk yourself out of a deal that was already moving toward you.

The buyer was thinking:

> “Yes, this makes sense.”

And your brain said:

> “Let's add another 90 seconds of explanation.”

Why?

Because you didn't trust the silence.


## The best salespeople understand something simple:


### Silence is not always a problem that needs to be fixed.

Sometimes the buyer is thinking.

Let them think.

Sometimes they're processing.

Let them process.

Sometimes they're deciding.

Don't interrupt the decision.

And sometimes...

they're waiting for you to stop talking. 😅

Knowing which one it is...

that's judgment.


## This is also why I don't want salespeople who can only sell in familiar situations.

I want someone who can walk into:

A new industry.

A new product.

A new buyer.

A new sales cycle.

A new objection.

A completely unfamiliar conversation...

and still think:

> “Okay. I don't have the answer yet. But I can figure out what's happening.”

That's a much more valuable skill than memorizing another 20 closing techniques.

Because techniques expire.

Markets change.

Buyer behavior changes.

Industries change.

Technology changes.

But the ability to think clearly inside a conversation...

stays valuable.


## And this is actually one of the reasons I built Authority Closers the way I did.

I don't want to create salespeople who collect techniques.

I don't want someone to leave training with:

> “I know 17 objection-handling methods.”

Great.

What happens when objection #18 walks through the door?

I want them to have something deeper.

I want them to be able to look at a situation and think:

> “What's really happening here?”

Then decide what to do.

That's what I call becoming an Authority Closer.

Not someone who has a script for everything.

Someone who can think when the script stops working.


## So here's my challenge to you.

The next time someone teaches you a sales rule, don't blindly accept it.

Ask:

> “When does this work?”

Then ask:

> “When doesn't it work?”

Then:

> “Why?”

And if the trainer can't answer the third question...

be careful.

Because memorizing a technique is easy.

Understanding its boundaries is where expertise begins.


## Here's a simple test I use now.

When someone says:

> “Always do this.”

Ask:

> “In which situation?”

When someone says:

> “Never do this.”

Ask:

> “Why?”

When someone says:

> “This works every time.”

Ask:

> “For which buyer?”

Those three questions can save you from a lot of bad sales advice.


## Because the biggest danger isn't learning a bad technique.

It's learning a good technique and using it at the wrong time.

That's much harder to detect.

Because the technique actually works.

Just...

not here.

And when it fails, you blame yourself.

> “Maybe I'm not good at sales.”

Maybe.

Or maybe...

you were given a hammer and told every sales problem was a nail.


## That's why I believe great sales training shouldn't create technique collectors.

It should create decision-makers.

People who know when to speak.

When to ask.

When to challenge.

When to listen.

When to follow up.

When to stop.

When to push.

When to step back.

When to use a framework.

And when to throw the framework away.

Because that's what real selling looks like.

Not:

> “Step 1. Step 2. Step 3. Close.”

But:

> “Here's the situation. What's actually happening?”

Then you decide.


### A script can tell you what to say.

A framework can tell you what to look for.

But judgment tells you what to do when reality doesn't follow the framework.

And reality...

never follows the framework perfectly.

That's why the best salespeople sometimes talk less.

They're not sitting there thinking:

> “What should I say next?”

They're thinking:

> “What is actually happening?”

And once you understand that...

you don't need to talk all the time.

Sometimes...

the smartest thing a salesperson can say is nothing.
