Communication
Communication
I used to think I was a good communicator.
Then I started working in sales.
And I realized...
I had confused speaking with communicating.

There's a big difference.
You can speak beautifully.
You can have excellent English.
You can have a great vocabulary.
You can be confident.
You can even be an amazing public speaker.
And still...
people may not understand what you're trying to say.
That's when I started looking at communication differently.

Here's the simplest definition of communication I've found.
Communication is not what you say.
Communication is what the other person understands.
That's it.
And once you really understand that...
a lot of communication advice starts looking different.
Because most people enter a conversation thinking:
“What do I want to say?”
I think a better communicator enters thinking:
“What does this person need to understand?”

One question starts with you.
The other starts with them.
That tiny shift changes everything.

Let me give you a strange example.
Imagine I tell two people:
“Our solution can increase your sales by 30%.”
Person A thinks:
“Interesting. How?”
Person B thinks:
“Sounds too good to be true.”
Person C thinks:
“I can't afford it.”
Person D thinks:
“If this is possible, why isn't everyone doing it?”

Same words.
Same speaker.
Same room.
Four completely different messages.

So did I communicate?
I spoke.
They heard me.
But what happened after that?
That's communication.
Because communication doesn't end at your mouth.
It ends in their interpretation.

This is one of the biggest mistakes I see in salespeople.
They think:
“But I explained it clearly.”
Okay.
But did the customer understand it?
Those aren't the same thing.
You can explain something perfectly from your perspective...
and completely fail to communicate it from their perspective.

Imagine I spend five minutes explaining an amazing feature.
The customer understands the feature.
And is still thinking:
“Okay... but why should I care?”

I communicated the feature.
I didn't communicate the value.
That's the difference.

And here's where communication becomes fascinating.
Sometimes people don't need you to explain better.
They need you to understand better.
That's a very uncomfortable thought for someone who thinks they're a good communicator.
Because now the problem isn't:
“How can I explain this?”
It's:
“Have I understood what matters to this person?”
And that's where listening comes in.

I don't think listening means sitting quietly.
A lot of salespeople think they're great listeners because they don't interrupt.
But I've seen this happen:
The customer is speaking.
The salesperson is nodding.
“Hmm.”
“Right.”
“Okay.”
But inside their head?
They're already preparing the next question.
They're not listening.
They're waiting for their turn to speak 😅

Real listening is different.
You're trying to understand:
What is this person actually saying?
What are they worried about?
What are they not saying?
Why does this matter to them?
What happened before?
What are they trying to avoid?
What would make this decision easier?
And sometimes...
what are they afraid will happen if they make the wrong decision?
…
Now you're listening.

There's a quote from Stephen R. Covey:
“Seek first to understand, then to be understood.”
The older I get, the more I realize how often people reverse this.
Especially in sales.
Customer:
“We're already working with another vendor.”
Salesperson:
“Actually, our solution is much better because...”
…
Why?
You haven't understood anything yet.
Why did they choose that vendor?
What are they happy with?
What isn't working?
What would make them change?
What would make them stay?

Now you have a conversation.
Before that...
you had a pitch.

And here's something I learned from sales that completely changed how I see communication.
The best communicators aren't necessarily the best speakers.
I've seen people who are extremely polished.
Perfect English.
Great vocabulary.
Excellent body language.
Very confident.
And yet...
they struggle to close.
Then I've seen someone with average English, simple vocabulary and no “speaker” personality...
close deals consistently.
Why?

Because one person is trying to sound impressive.
The other is trying to understand the buyer.
That observation changed something for me.
Communication isn't a performance.
It's a transfer of understanding.

This is also why I get slightly uncomfortable when businesses say:
“Our sales team needs better communication skills.”
Maybe.
But sometimes that's not the problem.
I hate when business owners ask soft-skill trainers to teach sales to their sales team.
Not because communication isn't important.
It absolutely is.
But...
communication skill ≠ sales skill.

A person can be:
Articulate.
Confident.
Polite.
Empathetic.
A great listener.
A fantastic public speaker.
And still be a terrible salesperson.
Why?

Because sales requires much more than communication.
You need to understand the market.
Diagnose problems.
Qualify opportunities.
Create value.
Position the solution.
Understand buyer psychology.
Handle objections.
Negotiate.
Follow up.
Ask for decisions.
Close.

Communication is one of the weapons.
It isn't the entire battlefield.

I've seen this mistake many times.
This is one of the reasons I don't teach communication as a standalone soft skill. In sales, communication has to help someone make a decision.

A salesperson can explain the product beautifully.
Then the customer says:
“Your price is too high.”
And everything collapses.
Why?
Because the salesperson knows how to speak...
but doesn't know how to think through the conversation.

They immediately start explaining.
“Our quality is better.”
“Our service is better.”
“Our competitors charge more.”
“We have great testimonials.”
“We give better support.”
Five minutes later...
the customer is still thinking:
“I just said it was expensive.” 😂
What if you asked:
“Compared to what?”

Now you're communicating.
You're investigating.
Maybe they're comparing you with a competitor.
Maybe they don't see enough value.
Maybe they genuinely don't have the budget.
Maybe they're testing you.
Maybe...
THEY DON'T TRUST YOU YET.

Same words.
Different problem.
And the response should change with the problem.

This is why I don't believe in communication scripts.
Scripts can help beginners.
But eventually, you need something more powerful:
judgment.
Because real conversations don't follow scripts.
People don't follow scripts.
A customer can say something you've never heard before.
What will you do then?

You can't open page 47 of your objection-handling manual. 😂
You need to understand the person well enough to think:
“What is actually happening here?”
Then respond.
That's a much higher level of communication.

And here's another thing people misunderstand.
Good communication doesn't mean saying more.
Sometimes the best communication is one sentence.
Sometimes it's one question.
Sometimes it's silence.
A customer says:
“This actually makes sense.”
And the salesperson immediately jumps in:
“Exactly! And let me explain why, because we also have...”
Stop.
😅
Maybe the buyer is already convincing themselves.
You don't always need to keep selling.
Sometimes...
your biggest communication mistake is continuing to communicate.
That's something nobody puts in a communication-skills course.

There's a sentence I love using in conversations:
“Tell me more.”
That's it.
Customer:
“We've tried training before and it didn't work.”
Most salespeople immediately defend themselves.
“Our training is different because...”
Why?

You don't know what happened yet.
Instead:
“Tell me more. What happened?”
And suddenly...
you learn about the previous trainer.
The implementation.
The team's resistance.
The management's expectations.
The lack of follow-up.
The real problem.
You didn't impress the customer.
You didn't give a brilliant presentation.
You simply became curious.
And CURIOSITY IS ONE OF THE MOST UNDERRATED COMMUNICATION SKILLS.

There's another distinction I really like.
Speaking is transferring words.
Communication is transferring meaning.
And sometimes...
Great communication is changing meaning.
A customer says:
“Our salespeople don't need training. They're experienced.”
A weak salesperson hears:
“No need for training.”
A better salesperson gets curious:
“What makes you feel that way?”

Maybe the owner believes experienced people don't need training.
Maybe previous training was useless.
Maybe the team is resistant.
Maybe the owner hasn't seen the cost of the problem yet.
The conversation changes.
Not because the salesperson used a magical phrase.

Because they didn't accept the first interpretation.
That's communication.

And this is where communication becomes extremely powerful in sales.
A salesperson walks into a meeting thinking:
“I need to explain my product.”
A better salesperson walks in thinking:
“I need to understand their situation.”

The first person is preparing a presentation.
The second person is preparing for a conversation.
And buyers can feel the difference.
Because nobody likes being talked at.
People want to feel:
“This person actually gets me.”

That's not manipulation.
That's understanding.

Think about a restaurant.
You ask the waiter:
“What should I order?”
He says:
“Everything is excellent.”
Not very useful.
Now imagine he says:
“If you like spicy food, get this. If you don't, avoid it. And honestly, you probably don't need the second starter. It's too much for four people.”
You trust that person more.
Why?
Because he wasn't trying to maximize the bill.
He was trying to improve your decision.
That's exactly how I think selling should feel.

And this connects to one of my strongest beliefs about sales.
“You are not in the business of making people like you.
You're in the business of HELPING THEM MAKE THE RIGHT DECISION.”
Sometimes that means asking an uncomfortable question.
Sometimes it means challenging their assumption.
Sometimes it means telling them:
“I don't think this is the right solution for you.”
Sometimes:
“You don't need this yet.”
That might actually make them trust you more.
Because you're no longer behaving like someone desperate to get a yes.
You're behaving like someone trying to get the right yes.
And sometimes...
the right decision is no.

That's why I don't think communication is about becoming more impressive.
I don't want to become the person who can use the biggest words in the room.
I don't want to sound intelligent.
I don't want people leaving a conversation thinking:
“Wow, Dipak speaks really well.”
I'd rather they leave thinking:
“He understood what I was trying to say.”

That's a completely different compliment.
And honestly...
it's a much more valuable one.

So when I think about communication now...
I ask:
Did I understand them?
Did they understand me?
Did I communicate what actually mattered?
Did I remove confusion or create more of it?
Did I listen for what wasn't being said?
Did I help them think more clearly?
Because that's what communication is to me.
Not vocabulary.
Not fancy English.
Not speaking fast.
Not speaking slowly.
Not having the perfect body language.
Not winning the conversation.
Creating understanding.
And when you bring that into sales...
something changes.
You stop trying to convince people.
You start helping them think.
You stop trying to sound impressive.
You start becoming useful.
You stop asking:
“What should I say next?”
And start asking:
“What should I understand next?”
That's the shift.
Because ultimately...
speaking is what happens from your side.
Communication is what happens between two minds.
And in sales...
the quality of that connection can be worth millions. 🔥

High-Ticket Sales
High-Ticket Sales
If someone is buying a ₹500 product, they're usually asking:
“Do I want this?”
But when you're selling something worth ₹50 lakh...
the question changes.
Now the buyer is also asking:
“Can I defend this decision?”
And I think that's the biggest difference between low-ticket and high-ticket sales.
It's not just the price.
It's the risk attached to the decision.

Think about it.
If you buy a ₹500 product and it turns out to be terrible...
You shrug.
Maybe you complain.
Maybe you never buy from them again.
Life moves on.
But imagine you're a founder who approves a ₹50 lakh solution for your company.
Six months later, it doesn't work.
Nobody walks into your office and says:
“Interesting experiment.”
Someone asks:
“Who recommended this?”
😂
Now the ₹50 lakh isn't just ₹50 lakh.
It's your reputation.
Your team's confidence.
Your credibility with your boss.
Your time.
Your opportunity cost.
Maybe even your career.
That's high-ticket sales.
The buyer isn't only buying your solution.
They're putting their reputation behind the decision.

And this is why I think high-ticket sales is misunderstood.
People think:
“If the deal is bigger, I need to become better at convincing.”
I think almost the opposite.
The bigger the deal, the less the buyer wants to feel sold.
Think about buying a ₹1,000 pair of shoes.
The salesperson can say:
“Sir, today only. Last piece. 20% discount.”
Fine.
But imagine someone trying that while you're deciding whether to invest ₹50 lakh in their business solution.
“Sir, today only. Last opportunity. Please decide now.”
You'd probably think:
“Why is this person more interested in my money than my decision?”
And that's where high-ticket selling gets interesting.

The bigger the deal...
the more expensive desperation becomes.
A desperate salesperson thinks:
“How do I close this?”
A strong high-ticket salesperson thinks:
“Is this actually the right decision for them?”
That difference changes everything.
Imagine you're in a sales conversation and the buyer clearly has a problem you can solve.
But after asking questions, you realize:
They're not ready.
The timing is wrong.
The team isn't prepared.
Or your solution isn't actually the best fit.
What do you do?
A desperate salesperson thinks:
“How can I overcome this?”
An advisor thinks:
“Maybe you shouldn't buy this yet.”
That sentence can be incredibly powerful.
Because now the buyer realizes:
“This person is willing to lose my money to protect my decision.”
That's trust.
Not friendliness.
Not charm.
Judgment.

And judgment is what high-ticket buyers are really paying attention to.
They aren't only asking:
“Is your product good?”
They're asking:
“Do you understand my situation?”
“Will you tell me if this isn't right for me?”
“Can I trust your recommendation?”
“Will you still be here after I pay?”
“Can I defend this decision internally?”
That's a much deeper definition of trust.
Because trust isn't:
“I like this salesperson.”
Trust is:
“I trust this person's judgment.”
And there's a massive difference.

This is why more information doesn't always create more sales.
I've seen salespeople turn high-ticket conversations into PowerPoint marathons.
40 slides.
Case studies.
Testimonials.
Features.
Benefits.
ROI.
Another demo.
Another case study.
And then:
“Any questions?”
😂
But here's the problem.
You can't solve a trust gap with another PowerPoint slide.
If the buyer's problem is:
“I don't know whether I can trust this decision.”
Giving them another 15 features doesn't solve it.
Sometimes it makes it worse.
Because now they have 15 more things to evaluate.

Think about this.
A buyer says:
“Your solution makes sense.”
That's not necessarily a buying signal.
They might genuinely believe your solution is excellent.
But they're still thinking:
“What if I'm wrong?”
And that's a completely different problem.
This is why a buyer can:
Love your product.
Like you.
Agree with your presentation.
Believe the ROI.
And still not buy.
Because they're not only evaluating the product.
They're evaluating the decision.

And high-ticket decisions rarely belong to one person.
This is another reason high-ticket sales is different.
The person sitting across from you might love your solution.
But then they have to talk to:
Finance.
The founder.
The CEO.
The operations team.
IT.
Procurement.
Their boss.
Maybe three other people you've never even met.
So you're not always selling to one person.
You're helping one person sell your solution internally.
That's a completely different skill.

Think about your champion.
Let's say a sales head loves your training program.
He thinks:
“This is exactly what my team needs.”
Great.
But tomorrow he's sitting in front of the CEO.
And the CEO asks:
“Why should we spend ₹15 lakh on this?”
Your sales champion now has to answer.
If you've only given him:
“Our program is amazing.”
he's in trouble.
But if you've helped him understand:
What problem are we solving?
What is that problem costing us?
Why now?
What happens if we don't solve it?
Why this solution?
Why this investment?
Now you've given him something much more valuable.
A business case.
That's why I say:
In high-ticket sales, your buyer often becomes your salesperson inside the organization.
Your job is to make them confident enough to carry your message into the room you aren't in.

And then there's my favorite sentence in sales:
“I'll think about it.”
Most salespeople immediately treat it like an objection.
I don't.
I treat it like an incomplete sentence.
Because what exactly are they going to think about?
The money?
The timing?
The risk?
The implementation?
Their boss?
Their team?
Your credibility?
Whether the problem is actually urgent?
Whether they can trust you?
So instead of immediately launching into another closing technique...
I'd rather understand:
“What part of the decision are you still uncomfortable with?”
That's a completely different conversation.
Now you're not fighting the objection.
You're finding the uncertainty.

And this is where I think salespeople make another mistake.
They hear:
“Your price is too high.”
And immediately start defending the price.
“Our quality is better.”
“Our competitors charge more.”
“You'll get 3X ROI.”
“We can offer a discount.”
Wait.
Why did the buyer say the price is too high?
Maybe they genuinely don't have the budget.
Maybe they don't see enough value.
Maybe they're comparing you with a cheaper alternative.
Maybe they're interested but not convinced.
Maybe they're testing you.
Maybe...
they don't trust you enough to risk the money.
Same sentence.
Completely different problem.
And if you respond to the wrong problem...
you can become extremely good at solving a problem the buyer never had.

That's why I don't believe high-ticket sales is about clever objection-handling lines.
You can memorize 50 responses.
But what happens when the buyer says something you've never heard before?
Real conversations don't follow scripts.
People don't follow scripts.
That's why I would rather train a salesperson to think than simply memorize what to say.
Because when you understand the psychology...
you can handle the conversation even when the conversation changes.

And here's another paradox.
The best high-ticket salesperson isn't necessarily the most persuasive person in the room.
Sometimes it's the person who is willing to say:
“I don't know.”
Or:
“I don't think we're the right fit.”
Or:
“You probably shouldn't buy this yet.”
Why?
Because high-ticket buyers aren't looking for someone who can make every problem disappear.
They're looking for someone whose judgment they can trust.
And sometimes good judgment means saying no.

I think this is where high-ticket sales becomes almost philosophical.
Your job isn't to convince someone to spend ₹50 lakh.
Your job is to help them decide whether spending ₹50 lakh is the right decision.
That's a completely different responsibility.
You're not trying to win against the buyer.
You're trying to help the buyer think clearly.
Because if the decision is wrong...
you don't win.
Even if you get the commission.
The buyer loses.
Your reputation loses.
And eventually, your business loses.

That's also why I believe high-ticket sales needs four kinds of risk to be addressed.
1. Financial risk
“Will I lose my money?”
2. Performance risk
“Will this actually work?”
3. Personal risk
“Will I look stupid if this goes wrong?”
4. Decision risk
“Can I defend this decision?”
And notice something.
Only the first one is directly about money.
That's why simply discounting your price doesn't solve high-ticket objections.
You might reduce financial risk...
while leaving the other three untouched.

So what actually reduces high-ticket resistance?
Not pressure.
Not fake urgency.
Not another discount.
Not another 40-slide presentation.
It's clarity.
The buyer needs clarity about:
The problem.
The cost of the problem.
The desired outcome.
The available options.
The risks.
The reason to act now.
And whether you are actually the right person to help.
When those things become clear...
the decision becomes easier.

And this connects to one of my strongest beliefs about sales.
You are not in the business of making people like you.
You are in the business of helping them make the right decision.
That becomes even more important when the ticket size gets bigger.
Because if someone buys a ₹500 product and makes a bad decision...
it's annoying.
If someone spends ₹50 lakh because you pressured them into it...
that's something else entirely.
High-ticket selling carries a higher responsibility.
And I think the best salespeople understand that.

So if you ask me what makes someone great at high-ticket sales...
It's not the ability to talk more.
It's not having the perfect closing line.
It's not knowing 37 objection-handling techniques.
It's the ability to sit across from someone and think:
“What does this person need to understand before they can make a confident decision?”
Then ask.
Listen.
Diagnose.
Challenge when necessary.
Tell the truth.
And sometimes...
walk away.
Because the best high-ticket salesperson isn't the person who can convince someone to spend ₹50 lakh.
It's the person who can help someone decide whether spending ₹50 lakh is the right decision in the first place.
And when buyers start trusting your judgment, not just your product...
you've stopped being a salesperson trying to close a deal.
You've become the person they trust to make the decision with them.

The previous version explained buyer psychology well. The upgraded version should make the reader experience buyer psychology while reading it.
The central idea I'm using:
Buyers don't simply decide whether they want your product. They decide whether they are comfortable being responsible for the decision.
That gives us a much stronger Dipak angle.
Buyer Psychology
I find buyer psychology fascinating because buyers rarely behave the way salespeople expect them to.
A salesperson thinks:
“If I explain the product properly, they'll understand the value.”
The buyer is thinking:
“If I buy this and it goes wrong, what happens to me?”
That's a completely different conversation.
And once you understand that...
sales starts looking very different.

Think about the last expensive thing you bought.
Not your coffee.
Not your ₹300 food delivery.
Something that actually mattered.
Maybe a car.
A house.
A business service.
A laptop.
A course.
An investment.
Did you only ask:
“Is this good?”
Probably not.
You probably asked:
“Can I trust this?”
“Is the price reasonable?”
“What if I make the wrong choice?”
“What if something goes wrong?”
“Should I compare it with something else?”
“What will other people think about this decision?”
And sometimes the most interesting question is:
“What happens if I do nothing?”
That's buyer psychology.

Here's the first thing I believe about buyers:
People don't just buy outcomes. They buy confidence in the outcome.
You can show someone an amazing product.
You can demonstrate the features.
You can show testimonials.
You can give them a discount.
You can explain the ROI.
And they can still say:
“I'll think about it.”
Why?
Maybe they don't need more information.
Maybe they need more certainty.
That's a very different problem.

And this is where salespeople often make a mistake.
The buyer says:
“I'm not sure.”
The salesperson thinks:
“I need to explain more.”
So they explain.
The buyer says:
“Hmm... okay.”
The salesperson explains even more.
The buyer says:
“Right.”
Now the salesperson is on slide 37.
😂
At some point, the salesperson has explained so much that the customer has more things to think about than when the conversation started.
Information doesn't automatically create certainty.
Sometimes...
information creates more uncertainty.

Imagine you're buying a car.
The salesperson tells you:
“Excellent mileage.”
“Premium interiors.”
“Advanced safety.”
“Powerful engine.”
“Latest technology.”
“Special financing.”
“Limited-time offer.”
Everything sounds great.
Then you ask:
“Which one would you buy if you were me?”
And suddenly...
the salesperson's entire energy changes.
Because now you aren't asking about the product.
You're asking for judgment.
That's what buyers often want at higher levels.
Not another feature.
A confident recommendation.

There's another thing I find fascinating.
A buyer can believe:
“This is a great product.”
and still decide:
“I'm not buying it.”
Both can be true.
Think about that.
Your product can be good.
Your price can be reasonable.
Your presentation can be excellent.
Your testimonials can be genuine.
And the buyer can still say no.
Because they're not only evaluating your product.
They're evaluating the decision.

And decisions have consequences.
Imagine you're a manager.
Your company spends ₹10 lakh on a new system because you recommended it.
Six months later...
the team doesn't use it.
The implementation is messy.
The results aren't there.
And now someone asks:
“Who approved this?”
Suddenly ₹10 lakh isn't just ₹10 lakh.
It's your reputation.
That's why high-value buyers often move slowly.
They're not necessarily trying to waste your time.
They're protecting themselves from making a decision they might have to defend later.
The bigger the consequence of being wrong, the more important trust becomes.

This is also why buyers don't always tell you the real objection.
A customer says:
“Your price is too high.”
Sounds like a price objection.
But what if it actually means:
“I don't see enough value.”
Or:
“I like the solution, but I'm not convinced you can deliver it.”
Or:
“I need to convince my boss.”
Or:
“I don't want to take the risk.”
Or simply:
“I don't trust you yet.”
Same sentence.
Different problem.
And if you answer the wrong problem...
you can become incredibly good at solving a problem the buyer never had.
😂

That's why I don't like objection-handling as a collection of clever replies.
A lot of sales training teaches:
Customer:
“Your price is too high.”
Salesperson:
“Compared to what?”
Or:
“If price wasn't an issue, would you buy?”
Or:
“What would it take to move forward today?”
Fine.
These can be useful.
But here's the bigger question:
Why did they say it?
That's the real skill.
Because the same words can come from completely different psychological states.
A great salesperson isn't just listening to the sentence.
They're listening for the meaning behind the sentence.

Here's something else buyers rarely say out loud.
“I don't want to look stupid.”
Think about it.
Nobody wants to be the person who made the terrible purchase.
Nobody wants to recommend a vendor who screws up.
Nobody wants to tell their boss:
“I thought this was going to work.”
and then spend six months explaining why it didn't.
So when someone buys from you...
they aren't only asking:
“Will this work?”
They're also asking:
“Will I regret this?”
That's a completely different psychological question.
And once you understand that...
you stop trying to make the buyer more excited.
You start making the decision feel safer.

This is why trust matters so much.
Trust isn't:
“I like this salesperson.”
That's only one small part of it.
Trust is:
“I believe this person understands my situation.”
“I believe they're telling me the truth.”
“I believe they won't disappear after taking my money.”
“I believe their recommendation makes sense.”
“I believe I can defend this decision if someone questions me.”
That's much deeper than being likable.
And this is one reason I strongly disagree with a certain idea in sales:
“Your job is to make the customer like you.”
No.
You are not in the business of making people like you.
You are in the business of helping them make the right decision.
Sometimes that means telling them:
“I don't think we're the right fit.”
And strangely...
that can increase trust.
Because now you're behaving like someone who is diagnosing, not chasing.

Think about a restaurant.
You're sitting with a group of friends.
You ask the waiter:
“What should we order?”
Imagine he says:
“Everything is excellent.”
Not very helpful.
Now imagine he says:
“If you like spicy food, get this. But if you don't, I'd avoid it. And honestly, you probably don't need the second starter. It's too much for four people.”
That person just increased your trust.
Why?
Because he wasn't trying to maximize the bill.
He was trying to improve the decision.
That's what good selling should feel like.

And here's where buyer psychology becomes really powerful.
The buyer has two conversations happening simultaneously.
Conversation #1:
“Do I want this?”
Conversation #2:
“Am I comfortable buying this?”
Salespeople spend most of their time on the first.
Features.
Benefits.
ROI.
Results.
Testimonials.
But sometimes the second conversation is the one actually stopping the sale.
“What if it doesn't work?”
“What if I choose the wrong vendor?”
“What if my team doesn't use it?”
“What if my boss disagrees?”
“What if I regret this?”
Now you're looking at the real psychology.

And there's an even stranger thing.
Sometimes buyers don't need you to convince them.
They need you to stop convincing them.
They already know they want the solution.
They just need to process the decision.
And the salesperson keeps talking.
Another feature.
Another testimonial.
Another case study.
Another reason.
Another discount.
At that point...
the salesperson isn't increasing confidence.
They're communicating:
“Please buy this. I really need you to buy this.”
And buyers are surprisingly good at detecting desperation.

That's why silence can be powerful.
Imagine the buyer says:
“This actually makes sense.”
And the salesperson immediately jumps in:
“Exactly! And let me tell you why, because we also have…”
Stop.
😂
Let the sentence breathe.
Sometimes the buyer is already convincing themselves.
Your job isn't always to push the decision forward.
Sometimes your job is simply to not get in the way of it.

This is why I think great salespeople are less like presenters...
and more like investigators.
They don't walk into the conversation thinking:
“What should I say?”
They walk in thinking:
“What should I discover?”
What is the real problem?
Why does it matter?
What have they tried?
Why didn't it work?
What happens if they do nothing?
Who else is involved?
What are they afraid of?
What would make this decision easy?
And sometimes...
what would make this decision impossible?
Those questions tell you much more than another product demo.

Because buyers don't buy simply because you created desire.
They buy when several things line up:
The problem matters.
The solution makes sense.
The value is clear.
The risk feels manageable.
The seller feels trustworthy.
The timing makes sense.
And most importantly...
the buyer feels comfortable owning the decision.
That's the part I think many salespeople miss.

So when I think about buyer psychology...
I don't think:
“How do I manipulate someone into buying?”
That's not selling.
I think:
“What does this person need to understand, believe and feel comfortable with before they can make a good decision?”
That question changes the entire conversation.
Because now you're not trying to win against the buyer.
You're trying to help the buyer think.
And when you can do that well...
something interesting happens.
You don't need to push the customer toward the decision.
You make the decision easier to make.
And that's when selling starts becoming less about persuasion...
and more about understanding people.

Startup
Exactly. The target is not “more startup knowledge.”
It is those little moments where a founder suddenly thinks:
“Wait... that's exactly what I've been doing.”
I've upgraded the chapter around that principle. I also deliberately changed the rhythm so it doesn't feel like the same formula as Communication, AI, or Personal Branding.
Startup
I think the word “startup” has become unnecessarily glamorous.
People hear startup and immediately imagine:
Funding.
Investors.
Pitch decks.
A cool office.
A founder with a MacBook sitting in a coffee shop. ☕😂
But a startup is actually much simpler than that.
A startup is a business trying to find a repeatable and scalable way to solve a real problem.
You're basically trying to answer four questions:
Does this problem actually exist?
Do people care enough about it to pay for a solution?
Can we solve it profitably?
Can we repeat this without everything depending on the founder?
And that last question is where many startups quietly start falling apart.

When I was in business consulting...
I remember reading somewhere, possibly a Harvard-related source, that around 95% of startups in India failed within their first five years.
Later, I remember seeing the figure being revised closer to 90%.
I wouldn't get obsessed with whether the number is exactly 90% or 95%.
The bigger point is this:
A huge number of startups don't make it.
And whenever I think about why...
I don't immediately think about funding.
I think about something much more uncomfortable.
Did they actually understand the market?
And...
Did the founder know how to sell?
Because you can have a brilliant idea.
You can have a beautiful product.
You can have a great team.
And still build a company nobody wants to buy from.
That's a painful lesson.
But it's a very expensive one if you learn it after spending two years building the company.

Here's something I think founders don't talk about enough.
Building a product can give you the illusion that you're making progress.
You can spend eight hours improving the product.
Nobody says no to you.
Nobody rejects you.
Nobody says:
“I don't need this.”
You go home feeling productive.
Sales is different.
You call someone.
They say no.
You call another person.
They say, “Send me details.”
You follow up.
They disappear.
Someone says:
“Your competitor is cheaper.”
Someone else says:
“We'll think about it.”
And suddenly...
you have to confront reality.
😂
That's one reason I think some founders accidentally hide behind their product.
They aren't necessarily lazy.
They're doing work.
But they're doing work that doesn't force the market to answer the uncomfortable question:
“Will anyone actually pay for this?”

That's why I believe research comes before scaling.
A founder can fall in love with an idea very quickly.
“I think people will love this.”
“My friends think it's amazing.”
“My team thinks it's brilliant.”
Great.
But here's the problem:
Your friends aren't the market.
And your customers don't owe you validation.
You can build something technically brilliant that solves a problem nobody considers urgent.
You can solve a real problem that people aren't willing to pay enough to solve.
You can build exactly what customers say they want...
and still discover that they don't actually buy it.
That's why I don't believe in asking only:
“Do you like this idea?”
Ask:
“Would you pay for it?”
Even better...
“Have you already spent money trying to solve this problem?”
Now you're getting somewhere.
Because there's a huge difference between a problem someone complains about...
and a problem someone is willing to pay to remove.
That's one of those things nobody tells you when you're excited about starting a company.

Here are some of the problems I see in startups:
Building before validating
Solving a problem nobody urgently wants solved
Talking to the wrong customers
Weak positioning
Poor pricing
Running out of cash
Hiring too early
Hiring the wrong people
Founder trying to do everything
No repeatable sales process
Depending too heavily on referrals
Building a product instead of building demand
Founder avoiding sales
Scaling before the business model is ready
And there's one problem hiding underneath many of these:
The founder hasn't figured out how to sell the thing yet.

This is why I strongly believe sales is the #1 skill a startup founder should learn.
Not because the founder should become the salesperson forever.
Quite the opposite.
The founder needs to understand sales deeply enough to eventually build a sales machine that doesn't depend on the founder.
Think about it.
Who is the founder selling to?
Customers.
Investors.
Employees.
Co-founders.
Partners.
Suppliers.
Early hires.
Sometimes even the family members asking:
“Beta, exactly what are you doing?” 😂
Everyone has to believe in something.
A founder is constantly selling.
Not always for money.
Sometimes you're selling a vision.
Sometimes you're selling confidence.
Sometimes you're selling an opportunity.
Sometimes you're selling the idea that the company is worth joining.
And sometimes...
you're selling yourself the reason to keep going.
So if you can't convince people...
how exactly are you planning to build a company?

And here's where I would tell every founder to read Sell Like Crazy.
Not because reading one sales book will magically make you a great salesperson.
It won't.
I recommend it because it made me think differently about where your time should actually go.
As a founder, everything looks important.
Product.
Marketing.
Finance.
Hiring.
Operations.
Technology.
Customer support.
Sales.
Your inbox has 47 things screaming:
“DO ME FIRST.” 😂
But the founder's job isn't to personally do all 47.
The founder's job is to figure out:
What only I can do?
What should I learn?
What should I delegate?
What should eventually become a system?
And sales is one of those things I don't think a founder should delegate too early.

Here's my rule:
Before you hire your first salesperson, learn how to sell the product yourself.
Why?
Because you need to discover the sales process before you can delegate the sales process.
You need to know:
Who buys?
Why do they buy?
Why don't they buy?
What objections keep coming up?
What words do customers actually use?
What part of your offer excites them?
What part scares them?
What makes them finally say yes?
Once you know that...
now you have something you can teach.
Otherwise, what are you going to tell your first salesperson?
“Here's the product. Here's your target. Go sell.”
😂
That's not delegation.
That's abandonment with a salary.

And here's another thing nobody tells founders.
Your first salesperson is often not there to discover your sales process.
Your founder should have already discovered a big part of it.
The salesperson's job should be to execute and improve the process, not spend six months figuring out whether your customers even understand why they should buy.
That's a completely different starting point.

Then comes the next challenge.
You finally have a salesperson who can sell.
Great.
But what happens if that salesperson leaves?
If tomorrow your best salesperson resigns...
and your revenue falls off a cliff...
you don't have a sales machine.
You have a sales dependency.
That's why building a strong sales team is not just about hiring talented closers.
You need:
A clear target market.
A clear offer.
A clear sales process.
Qualification.
Follow-up.
Objection handling.
Training.
Coaching.
Measurement.
And a system that allows someone else to reproduce what your best salesperson does.
That's when sales becomes scalable.

This is where I see founders make another interesting mistake.
They blame the salesperson.
“She's not closing.”
“He isn't aggressive enough.”
“They don't follow up properly.”
Maybe.
But maybe the salesperson isn't the real problem.
Maybe the leads are wrong.
Maybe the offer is weak.
Maybe the positioning is unclear.
Maybe the price doesn't make sense.
Maybe the founder hasn't given the salesperson enough proof.
Maybe the sales process is broken.
Maybe...
the market simply doesn't want what you've built.
And if the founder doesn't understand sales, every one of these problems looks like:
“My salesperson is bad.”
That's dangerous.
Because you can fire five salespeople...
and keep the real problem.

And that's why I don't think founders should be afraid of sales.
I've heard:
“I'm not a salesperson.”
Okay.
But you're a founder.
You're going to have to convince someone of something.
Eventually.
You don't need to become loud.
You don't need to become aggressive.
You don't need to turn into the person who talks for 20 minutes without taking a breath. 😂
You need to learn how to:
Understand people.
Ask better questions.
Find real problems.
Communicate value.
Handle uncertainty.
Build trust.
Ask for decisions.
That's sales.
And those skills don't just help you acquire customers.
They help you build the company.

So if you're building a startup, here's what I'd focus on.
Don't start by asking:
“How do I scale?”
Ask:
“Have I found a problem people will actually pay me to solve?”
Then:
“Can I sell the solution myself?”
Then:
“Can I make that sales process repeatable?”
Then:
“Can I teach someone else to do it?”
And only then:
“How aggressively can I scale this?”
Because scaling a broken process doesn't make the problem smaller.
It makes the problem bigger.

A startup is basically one giant sales conversation.
You convince someone to invest.
You convince someone to join.
You convince someone to buy.
You convince someone to partner.
You convince someone to trust you.
And eventually...
you need to convince the market that the problem you're solving is important enough to pay you for.
So yes...
Build the product.
Build the technology.
Build the team.
Build the company.
But before you try to build a billion-dollar startup...
make sure you can answer one very simple question:
“Can I get someone to pay me?”
Because before you can scale a business...
you first have to sell one.

Personal Branding
Personal Branding
I am a big fan of people who build personal brands.
Not because I think everyone needs 100,000 followers.
Not because everyone needs to become an influencer.
And definitely not because everyone needs to make a reel while pointing at text appearing on the screen. 😂
I like personal branding for a much simpler reason:
When you become a brand, people don't have to rediscover you every time you enter a room.
They already know what you stand for.
They already have some idea of how you think.
They already associate your name with something.
And that...
is incredibly powerful.

Think about what AI is doing to the world.
AI can write.
AI can design.
AI can create presentations.
AI can make videos.
AI can analyze information.
AI can generate ideas.
It can produce a lot of things that previously required a human being.
And it's getting better very quickly.
So here's the question I keep coming back to:
When everyone can create, what makes people choose you?
Because soon, producing content won't be impressive.
Producing a presentation won't be impressive.
Writing an article won't be impressive.
Creating a decent marketing campaign won't be impressive.
AI will make all of that easier.
So what becomes scarce?
Trust.
Reputation.
Experience.
Point of view.
Personality.
Proof.
And that's exactly where personal branding becomes powerful.
Gary Vaynerchuk has been saying for years that your personal brand is essentially your reputation. And I think AI is making that idea even more important.
Because when machines can create almost anything...
people start looking for the person behind the creation.

Here's a strange thing I've noticed about the world.
There was a time when people from villages wanted to come to cities.
They wanted better restaurants.
Better hotels.
More luxurious experiences.
More modern lifestyles.
The city represented progress.
And now?
People living in those same cities are paying money to go to village-style restaurants.
They want trees.
Mud walls.
Local food.
Simple seating.
Peace.
Sometimes they'll sit on the floor and happily pay ₹1,000 for food they could probably make at home for ₹150. 😂
Why?
Because when everything becomes standardized...
authenticity becomes valuable.
That's exactly what I think is going to happen with personal brands.

AI will make content abundant.
But it won't make you abundant.
Your experiences are still yours.
Your mistakes are yours.
Your stories are yours.
Your way of connecting two unrelated ideas is yours.
Your sense of humor is yours.
Your beliefs are yours.
Your reputation is yours.
And your perspective has something AI doesn't automatically have:
a history behind it.
You didn't just read about your industry.
You lived through things.
You made decisions.
Some worked.
Some didn't.
You met people.
You lost deals.
You won deals.
You changed your mind.
You learned.
That history creates something very valuable:
context.
And context creates trust.

But there's a big misunderstanding about personal branding.
People think personal branding means:
“Promote yourself.”
No.
That's self-promotion.
Personal branding is different.
Self-promotion says:
“Look at me.”
Personal branding says:
“This is what I believe.”
Self-promotion says:
“I'm the best.”
Personal branding says:
“Here's how I see the problem differently.”
One asks for attention.
The other earns association.
That's the difference.

Think about the people you remember.
Someone says:
Electric cars.
A few names immediately come to mind.
Someone says:
Technology.
Certain people appear.
Someone says:
Cricket.
You don't need a list.
Your brain already has associations.
That's what a personal brand really is.
An association in somebody else's mind.
You hear the name...
and something comes with it.
That's the goal.
Not fame.
Association.

And this is where personal branding becomes very interesting for business.
Let's imagine two consultants.
Consultant A is extremely good.
Twenty years of experience.
Amazing results.
But almost nobody knows him.
Consultant B has similar expertise.
But he has spent years sharing his thinking publicly.
People know what he believes.
They've watched him explain problems.
They've seen his case studies.
They've heard him speak.
They've consumed his ideas.
They know his personality.
Who do you think gets the easier first conversation?
Usually...
the second person.
Not necessarily because he's better.
Because he's known.
And being known reduces friction.

This is one reason I love personal branding.
It can reduce the amount of explaining you have to do.
Imagine meeting someone for the first time and saying:
“Hi, I'm a sales trainer.”
Now compare that with:
“Oh, you're Dipak Vishwakarma? I've seen your content on sales.”
That's a completely different conversation.
In the second situation...
the sales conversation has already partially started before you entered the room.
That's leverage.

And this is especially important if you're moving from a job into business.
I remember reading The Millionaire Fastlane by M.J. DeMarco, and one idea from the book stayed with me.
The broader philosophy of the book is about escaping the traditional model of trading your time for money and building something that creates leverage.
That made me think about personal branding differently.
Because imagine spending 15 years becoming extremely good at something...
but every time you change jobs or start something new, you have to introduce yourself from zero.
Now imagine those same 15 years were spent building a reputation around your expertise.
People know you.
They trust your thinking.
They know what you stand for.
They've seen your work.
They know the problems you solve.
Now you don't start from zero.
You carry your reputation with you.
And that's a powerful asset.

Your personal brand can outlive your business.
This is probably my favorite part.
Let's say today you build Company A.
You become known for a particular problem.
People associate you with it.
Five years later...
you decide to start Company B.
The company is new.
The logo is new.
The website is new.
The product is new.
But...
you aren't new.
You already have relationships.
You already have attention.
You already have credibility.
You already have people who are willing to listen.
You already have an audience.
So you're not entering the market as a complete stranger.
You're walking in carrying something you've spent years building.
Your reputation.

Think about the difference.
Entrepreneur A starts a new business with:
₹0 audience.
₹0 reputation.
₹0 trust.
Entrepreneur B starts the same business with:
50,000 people who know his work.
10,000 people who regularly consume his ideas.
Hundreds of people who have interacted with him.
A strong reputation in his industry.
Same business.
Same product.
Same market.
Completely different starting line.
That's personal branding.

And there's another reason I believe it matters.
It can change what you're able to charge.
Not because personal branding magically makes you more valuable.
It doesn't.
You still need to be good.
You still need results.
You still need to create real value.
But when people can see your expertise before they speak to you...
they don't evaluate you only on time.
They start evaluating you on judgment.
And that's a very different game.
You stop selling:
“10 hours of my time.”
And start selling:
“Here's what I've learned from doing this for years, and here's what I think you should do.”
The market can pay a premium for that.
Because sometimes people aren't paying for the hour.
They're paying to avoid the mistake.

But personal branding has another advantage I love.
It forces you to think.
If you know that people are going to see your opinion...
you can't casually have an opinion about everything.
You start asking:
“What do I actually believe?”
“Why do I believe it?”
“What have I experienced that supports it?”
“Where do I disagree with the conventional thinking?”
And slowly...
you stop becoming just a professional who does something.
You become someone with a point of view about that thing.
That's when a personal brand starts getting interesting.

And that's exactly why I started thinking about my own brand differently.
I didn't want people to know me simply as:
“A sales trainer.”
There are plenty of sales trainers.
I wanted people to understand:
How I think about sales.
How I think about buyers.
How I think about communication.
How I think about founders.
How I think about technology.
How I think about personal development.
How I think about the future of selling.
Because I don't want my reputation to be built only around the service I sell.
I want it to be built around the ideas I stand for.
That's a much bigger asset.

And here's the irony.
People often think:
“I'll build my personal brand after my business becomes successful.”
I think that's backwards.
Your personal brand can help your business become successful.
It can bring attention.
It can create trust.
It can open conversations.
It can attract employees.
It can attract partnerships.
It can make hiring easier.
It can create opportunities you weren't actively looking for.
And sometimes...
someone will reach out to you simply because they have been watching your thinking for months.
You didn't sell them that day.
You sold them long before they contacted you.
That's the compounding effect.

But don't confuse followers with a personal brand.
You can have one million followers...
and nobody knows what you actually stand for.
That's not necessarily a strong personal brand.
You can have 10,000 followers...
and become the first person people think about when a specific problem comes up.
I'd take the second one.
Because personal branding isn't really about:
How many people know you.
It's about:
What do they know you for?
That's the question.

And this is why I think the AI era will make personal brands even more valuable.
When content was scarce...
creating content gave you attention.
When content becomes abundant...
being trusted becomes the advantage.
AI can create another sales article.
Another video.
Another presentation.
Another email.
Another business idea.
But when someone asks:
“Who should I trust with this?”
They're not looking for the best-generated answer.
They're looking for a person.
A reputation.
A track record.
A point of view.
Someone whose thinking they recognize.

So if you ask me whether you should build a personal brand...
my answer is simple:
Yes.
But don't build it just to become famous.
Build it so your expertise has a name attached to it.
Build it so people know what you stand for.
Build it so your reputation travels further than your calendar.
Build it so if you start another business five years from now...
you don't have to introduce yourself again.
And most importantly...
build something that is difficult for AI, competitors or anyone else to copy:
a reputation that belongs to you.
Because businesses can change.
Products can change.
Industries can change.
Technology can change.
But if you've built a strong personal brand...
you don't have to start from zero every time your life changes direction.

AI
AI
I have a very simple view about AI.
AI is neither good nor bad.
It's a tool.
And like almost every powerful tool humans have created, its impact depends on who is using it, how they're using it, and what boundaries we put around it.
A hammer can build a house.
The same hammer can break a window.
We don't look at the hammer and say:
“This thing is evil.”
We look at the person holding it.
I think AI deserves the same conversation.

But here's where I get excited.
I don't see AI only as a threat.
I see it as a massive economic opportunity.
And we've seen this movie before.
There was a time when everyone wanted an Engineering degree.
Then came the MBA boom.
Around 2017, suddenly everyone was talking about Digital Marketing.
Then COVID happened...
and the coaching and online education industry exploded.
And now?
AI.
Everywhere.
Every industry is asking:
“How is AI going to change my business?”
And I don't think this is just another temporary hype cycle.
AI is going to keep disturbing industries.
Some businesses will disappear.
Some will become dramatically more efficient.
Some new businesses will appear that we can't even imagine properly yet.
And some people will make a ridiculous amount of money solving problems that AI suddenly makes possible to solve.
That's how technology works.
It doesn't politely enter an industry.
It rearranges the furniture. 😂

Here's the opportunity I see.
Every time a new technology arrives, people ask:
“Will this technology take my job?”
I think a better question is:
“What can this technology allow me to do that I couldn't do before?”
That's where the money usually is.
When digital marketing became popular, some people complained about how advertising was changing.
Others learned Facebook Ads.
SEO.
Funnels.
Email marketing.
Content.
They didn't just survive the change.
They built businesses around it.
AI is creating another wave like that.
And if you're early enough...
you don't have to fight the wave.
You can learn to ride it.

And then I hear:
“But AI is going to take people's jobs.”
Maybe.
Some jobs will disappear.
Some jobs will shrink.
Some jobs will change completely.
I'm not going to give you the comfortable answer and say:
“Don't worry. AI will never replace you.”
That's irresponsible.
Instead, I'll tell you something much less comfortable:
Learn AI.
Seriously.
If your job can be dramatically improved, automated or replaced by someone who knows how to use AI...
and you refuse to learn it...
why would the market protect you?
Be selfish.
At least initially.
Help yourself first.
Learn the tool.
Understand what it can do.
Figure out how your work is changing.
Become the person who knows how to use AI rather than the person competing against AI.
Then...
once you're standing on solid ground, help other people do the same.
You cannot save people who are refusing to move.

But there's something even more interesting happening.
AI is making knowledge cheaper.
Think about it.
You can ask AI to explain a concept.
Summarize a book.
Write a proposal.
Create a presentation.
Analyze data.
Give you ideas.
Help you research.
Five years ago, some of these things required another person.
Now you can do them in minutes.
So what happens when information becomes abundant?
Information becomes less valuable by itself.
And that changes what becomes valuable.
Judgment.
Experience.
Taste.
Trust.
Execution.
Relationships.
Decision-making.
And one thing I care about enormously:
Practice.
Because knowing something and being able to do something are two completely different things.

You can ask AI how to play cricket.
It can explain the stance.
The grip.
The footwork.
The backlift.
It can even analyze a video of your batting.
And yet...
you can walk onto the pitch and get bowled first ball. 🏏😂
Why?
Because knowledge isn't practice.
You can understand something intellectually...
without being able to perform it under pressure.
And sales is exactly like that.
You can watch 100 videos about objection handling.
You can memorize every closing framework.
You can understand buyer psychology.
You can even ask AI:
“What should I say when the customer says your price is too high?”
And AI will happily give you 20 answers.
But when the customer actually looks at you and says:
“Your competitor is 30% cheaper.”
Your brain doesn't get a multiple-choice question.
You have to respond.
That's a skill.
And skills are built through repetition.

This is where AI gets really interesting for sales.
AI can make huge parts of the sales process easier.
Lead qualification.
Research.
CRM updates.
Follow-ups.
Email sequences.
Appointment setting.
Proposal creation.
Call summaries.
Personalized outreach.
Data analysis.
And follow-up...
My God. 😂
Salespeople have spent years saying:
“I'll call them tomorrow.”
AI doesn't forget tomorrow.
It doesn't get tired.
It doesn't have a bad Monday.
It doesn't suddenly remember the prospect three weeks later while eating dinner.
It follows the process.
And that can completely change the economics of selling.

Think about a low-ticket business.
You have a good funnel.
Good messaging.
Good qualification.
Automated follow-ups.
Good nurturing.
The prospect keeps receiving the right message at the right time.
Some low-ticket products can potentially get sold with very little human intervention.
That's powerful.
But...
high-ticket sales are different.

Because high-ticket sales eventually comes down to trust.
If you're asking someone to spend ₹5,000...
they might take a chance.
₹50,000?
They'll think more.
₹5 lakh?
Now they want confidence.
₹50 lakh?
Now they want to know exactly who they're dealing with.
Because when the financial risk increases...
the emotional risk increases too.
Nobody wants to be the person sitting in a boardroom six months later saying:
“Yeah... I made the wrong decision.”
That's why high-ticket buyers don't just evaluate your product.
They evaluate you.
Your judgment.
Your credibility.
Your ability to understand their situation.
Your ability to handle complexity.
Your confidence when things don't go according to plan.
And that is where I think humans still have a huge advantage.

AI can give you information.
But trust is different.
AI can write the perfect follow-up.
But it doesn't have the relationship you've built over five conversations.
AI can analyze the buyer's words.
But a great salesperson notices:
“Something changed when you mentioned the price.”
AI can suggest ten responses to an objection.
But an experienced salesperson may realize:
“This isn't actually a price objection.”
It's fear.
Internal politics.
Lack of urgency.
Or simply...
they don't trust us yet.
That's why I don't think the future of sales is:
Human vs AI.
I think it's:
Human + AI vs Human without AI.
That's a much more interesting competition.

And this brings me to something we're building at Authority Closers.
For years, one of the biggest challenges I've had while training salespeople has been practice.
Teaching the concept is one thing.
Getting someone to actually practice it properly is completely different.
Think about what normally happens.
I teach a salesperson how to handle:
“Your price is too high.”
Great.
Now they need to practice.
So I become the customer.
Then my team becomes the customer.
Then another student becomes the customer.
And I have to keep changing the scenario.
Different objection.

nothing.

the buyer didn't say no.

Get the customer to say yes.

Answer it.

Solve it.

Explain more.

Maybe I should have given a better example.

The customer had nodded.
They had agreed.
They had asked questions.
They had even said:
“This looks good.”
So why didn't they buy?
It took me time to understand something very simple:
A buyer agreeing with you is not the same as a buyer being comfortable making the decision.
That changed the way I looked at sales.

This is where I think salespeople misunderstand buyers.
A salesperson often sees only two possibilities:
Yes
or
No.
But human beings don't always operate like that.
There's a huge space between them.
And that space is called:
uncertainty.
The buyer may like your product.
They may believe you can help.
They may agree with your pricing.
They may even tell you:
“This makes complete sense.”

they don't buy.

You think again.
You might even go back home and say:
“I'll decide tomorrow.”
😂
The problem isn't that you don't want to go anywhere.
The problem is that you're not confident about which direction to take.
That's how I see many stalled sales conversations.
The buyer isn't necessarily walking away.
They're standing at the crossroads.
And here's the mistake salespeople make.
They see the buyer standing still and start shouting:
“GO LEFT! GO LEFT! GO LEFT!”
That's not helping.
That's pressure.

So what exactly are buyers uncertain about?
This is where it gets interesting.
Because uncertainty isn't always:
“I don't know if I want your product.”
It can be much deeper.
They might be thinking:
“Will this actually work for me?”
“What if it doesn't?”
“Am I paying too much?”
“Should I compare another option?”
“What if I wait?”
“What if I make the wrong decision?”
“What will my boss say?”
“Can my team actually implement this?”
“What happens if I recommend this and it fails?”
Now you start seeing the real problem.
The buyer isn't simply evaluating your product.
They're evaluating the consequences of choosing it.

And here's a distinction I think salespeople should understand.
A buyer can have desire without having decision confidence.
They can want the outcome...
without being comfortable with the decision.
Think about buying a car.
You might absolutely love the car.
You might think:
“This is exactly what I want.”
But then you see the price.
You think about the EMI.
You think about maintenance.
You think:
“Maybe I should check one more model.”
You go home.
You sleep on it.
You wake up.
You still want the car.
But you're not ready to buy it.
Desire is there.
Decision confidence isn't.
That's a very different situation from rejection.

This is why “I'll think about it” is such an interesting sentence.
Most salespeople hear:
“I'll think about it.”
and immediately think:
Objection.
I hear:
Incomplete sentence.
Because what exactly are they going to think about?
The price?
The timing?
Your credibility?
The competition?
The risk?
Their boss?
Their spouse?
Their team?
Whether they actually need it?
Whether now is the right time?
Or...
whether they trust themselves to make the decision?
That's why I don't like immediately throwing an objection-handling line at them.
First understand:
“What exactly is still unclear for you?”
Now you've got something to work with.

And here's where many salespeople accidentally make uncertainty worse.
The buyer says:
“I'm not sure.”
The salesperson thinks:
“I need to explain more.”
So they start talking.
Another feature.
Another testimonial.
Another case study.
Another ROI calculation.
Another example.
Another reason.
Five minutes later...
the salesperson has given the buyer more information but less clarity.
That's a dangerous combination.
Because information and clarity are not the same thing.
You can give someone 50 facts...
and leave them more confused than when they started.

Mexican.
Punjabi.
Korean.
Fine dining.
Street food.
Buffet.
Café.
Rooftop.
And then says:
“You decide.” 😂

You needed a recommendation.
Sometimes buyers are exactly the same.
They don't need another feature.
They need someone who understands their situation well enough to say:
“Based on what you've told me, this is what I would do.”
That's a completely different kind of selling.

But there is another type of uncertainty that is even more dangerous.
The uncertainty the buyer doesn't tell you about.
A buyer might say:
“Your price is high.”
And you assume the problem is price.
But what if the real thought is:
“I don't know whether I trust you enough to spend this much.”
Or:
“I'm interested, but I don't know whether my team will use it.”
Or:
“I need to convince my boss.”
Or:
“I don't want to be responsible if this doesn't work.”
Now you've got a completely different problem.
And this is why I believe great salespeople don't listen only to what the buyer says.
They listen for what the buyer might be trying to figure out.

Here's something else I've noticed.
Buyers often become quieter when they're uncertain.
And salespeople often become louder.
That's a terrible combination. 😂
Buyer:
“Hmm... I'm not sure.”
Salesperson:
“Actually, let me explain one more thing...”
Buyer:
“Okay.”
Salesperson:
“And we also have this feature...”
Buyer:
“Right.”
Salesperson:
“Plus, we've worked with companies like yours...”
The salesperson feels productive.
The buyer is becoming mentally exhausted.
And then the salesperson wonders:
“Why did they suddenly go cold?”

Maybe you overwhelmed them while they were trying to think.

You'd probably say:
“Can we first figure out what's wrong?”

What exactly are they uncertain about?

“What would you need to feel comfortable making this decision?”

“What can I do to close this today?”

The first is about their decision.
The second is about your target.

Maybe you made a promise that sounded unrealistic.
Maybe you couldn't explain something simply.
Maybe you pushed too hard.
Maybe you avoided a difficult question.
Maybe you were so desperate to close that the buyer started wondering:
“Why does he need my yes this badly?”
That thought can destroy a sale very quickly.

“If the customer understands my solution, they'll buy.”

Maybe they understand it perfectly.
Maybe your solution is excellent.
Maybe your ROI is fantastic.
But the buyer is thinking:
“It's a good idea. Just not important enough right now.”
That's not a product problem.
That's an urgency problem.
And no amount of explaining can manufacture urgency that doesn't exist.
Sometimes the right answer is not:
“How do I convince them?”
It's:
“Is this problem actually important enough for them to solve right now?”
That's a much better question.

There's another reason buyers stay uncertain.
They don't want to regret the decision.
This becomes especially important when the purchase is expensive.
Because nobody wants to be the person who says six months later:
“I knew I shouldn't have bought this.”
So the buyer isn't only asking:
“Will this work?”
They're also asking:
“What if I'm wrong?”
That's a completely different psychological conversation.
And the bigger the decision...
the more important that question becomes.

This is why I don't believe the salesperson's job is to eliminate uncertainty.
That's impossible.
Every meaningful decision has some uncertainty.
Buying a car has uncertainty.
Hiring an employee has uncertainty.
Starting a business has uncertainty.
Investing money has uncertainty.
Even choosing a restaurant has uncertainty.
😂
You don't need to create a world where the buyer feels:
“There is absolutely zero risk.”
That's not believable.
Your job is to help them understand:
What is the actual risk?
What isn't a real risk?
What do we know?
What don't we know?
What happens if we do nothing?
And based on all of that... is this still the right decision?
That's much more honest.

And sometimes...
the right decision is no.
This is important.
If after understanding the situation you genuinely believe your solution isn't right...
say it.
If the timing is wrong...
say it.
If they don't have the resources to implement it...
say it.
If another solution is better...
say it.
Because you're not in the business of manufacturing yeses.
You're in the business of helping people make the right decision.
And ironically...
when you're willing to say no,
your yes becomes more valuable.

So the next time a buyer says:
“I'll think about it.”
Don't immediately think:
“How do I overcome this objection?”

Ask yourself:
What exactly are they uncertain about?
Is it the money?
The value?
The timing?
The risk?
The implementation?
The trust?
The urgency?
The internal approval?
Or something they haven't told me yet?
Because the deal may not be dead.
It may simply be stuck.
And there's a huge difference between the two.
A dead deal needs to be replaced.
A stuck deal needs to be understood.

That's probably the biggest shift I've made in the way I look at selling.
When I was starting out, I thought a good salesperson was someone who could remove every objection.
Today, I think a good salesperson is someone who can discover the real uncertainty behind the objection.
That's a completely different skill.
And honestly...
it's made selling much less frustrating for me.
Because I no longer have to walk out of every meeting thinking:
“What did I say wrong?”
Sometimes the better question is:
“What was the buyer still trying to figure out?”
That question gives you something useful.
It gives you something to investigate.
Something to learn.
Something to improve.

The buyer may not be saying no.

waiting for enough clarity to choose a road.
And the salesperson who understands that doesn't push harder.
They help the buyer see the road clearly.

“Don't look stupid.”
So I'd try to answer.
Sometimes I'd give a longer explanation.
Sometimes I'd give an answer that sounded confident even though I wasn't completely sure.
And after the conversation, I'd replay it in my head.
Sometimes I'd think:
“Why was I trying so hard to prove myself?”
That question bothered me.
Because I realized:
I wasn't trying to help the buyer make a decision.
I was trying to protect my image as the expert.
And those two things can pull you in completely different directions.

Here's a simple test.
A buyer asks you something and you genuinely don't know the answer.
What do you say?
Option 1:
Start talking.
“Actually, our system is designed to handle multiple integrations and we've worked with several platforms...”
You keep going.
You sound confident.
But you're hoping the buyer doesn't notice that you never actually answered the question. 😂
Option 2:
“I don't know the answer to that. Let me check and come back to you rather than give you the wrong information.”
Which one creates more trust?
For me, it's obvious.
Because the second salesperson just communicated:
“I'd rather be accurate than impressive.”
That's a powerful signal.

And that's when I started seeing trust differently.
Trust isn't:
“This person sounds confident.”
Trust is:
“I believe this person's judgment.”
Those are not the same.
A confident person can be completely wrong.
An experienced person can still give bad advice.
A charming person can have a hidden agenda.
A salesperson with 500 client logos can still recommend the wrong solution.
So credentials help.
Experience helps.
Confidence helps.
But eventually the buyer is asking something much more personal:
“If I follow this person's recommendation, do I believe I'll be okay?”
That's trust.

And here's where authority gets misunderstood.
We're constantly told:
“Establish your authority.”
So salespeople start listing achievements.
“I've worked with 200 companies.”
“We're a market leader.”
“We've generated ₹X crore.”
“I've been doing this for 15 years.”
“Here's our client list.”
Great.
You've established that you've done things.
But the buyer is still wondering:
“Do you understand MY situation?”
That's a different question.
You can have 500 clients...
and completely misunderstand the 501st.

Imagine a business owner tells you:
“We need more leads.”
A salesperson who wants the deal might immediately say:
“Absolutely. We can generate more leads.”
Problem solved.
Sale moving forward.
Except...
maybe the company already has enough leads.
Maybe the sales team is terrible at following up.
Maybe the leads are coming in but nobody is qualifying them.
Maybe the offer isn't converting.
Maybe the salespeople aren't asking for the sale.
Now imagine you say:
“Before we talk about getting more leads, can I understand what happens to the leads you're already getting?”
You just slowed the sale down.
But you may have increased trust.
Because you didn't blindly accept the problem statement.
You diagnosed it.
And diagnosis communicates something powerful:
“I'm not going to recommend something until I understand what you actually need.”

Here's another paradox.
Sometimes the fastest way to build trust is to risk losing the sale.
Imagine a business owner wants to launch your training program immediately.
You know their sales team is overloaded.
You know nobody has allocated time for practice.
You know the managers aren't going to reinforce what gets taught.
You could still say:
“Absolutely. Let's start next Monday.”
Deal closed.
Commission secured.
Or you could say:
“Honestly, I don't think you should start next Monday. Your team isn't ready to implement this properly. Give me two weeks to help you prepare the managers first.”
You just delayed your own revenue.
But you also told the buyer:
“I'm protecting the outcome, not chasing the invoice.”
That is a completely different relationship.

And buyers notice this.
They may not consciously think:
“Wow, this salesperson has demonstrated integrity.”
It's usually much simpler.
They just get a feeling:
“Something about this person feels different.”
Why?
Because every interaction is giving them evidence.
Do you tell the truth?
Do you keep your word?
Do you admit mistakes?
Do you challenge them when necessary?
Do you recommend what they actually need?
Do you still care after the payment?
Do you say no when no is the right answer?
Slowly...
the buyer forms a conclusion.
“I trust this person.”
Trust isn't something you announce.
It's something they conclude.

This is why I don't like the phrase:
“Trust me.”
😂
Whenever someone says:
“Trust me, sir.”
My brain immediately goes:
Why are you asking me to trust you?
If someone has to repeatedly tell you they're trustworthy...
something has gone wrong.
It's like meeting someone at a networking event and within five minutes they say:
“You can trust me completely.”
Buddy...
I don't even know your surname yet. 😂
Trust needs evidence.

And here's another thing that destroys trust:
Agreeing with everything.
Some salespeople are so desperate to maintain rapport that every sentence from the buyer gets:
“Absolutely.”
Customer:
“I think we need more leads.”
“Absolutely.”
Customer:
“I think our competitors are doing better.”
“Absolutely.”
Customer:
“I think we should cut the price.”
“Absolutely.”
At some point the buyer is thinking:
“Does this person actually have a brain of their own?” 😂
Sometimes disagreement is valuable.
Not because you should argue with customers.
But because an expert should have a point of view.
If you believe the buyer is wrong...
tell them.
Respectfully.
Explain why.
Give them the evidence.
That's not bad rapport.
That's judgment.

And judgment is what I think buyers really want from experienced salespeople.
A beginner salesperson asks:
“What should I say to convince them?”
An experienced salesperson asks:
“What is actually the right recommendation here?”
That difference is massive.
The first person is protecting the sale.
The second person is protecting the decision.
And guess who I'd rather buy from?
The second person.

There's another uncomfortable truth.
Sometimes...
you are the reason the buyer doesn't trust you.
Not because you lied.
You may have been completely honest.
But maybe you were trying too hard.
Too much confidence.
Too many claims.
Too many testimonials.
Too many “we're the best” statements.
Too many promises.
Too much urgency.
Too much talking.
The buyer starts feeling:
“Why is this person working so hard to convince me?”
And once that happens...
even genuine statements can start sounding like sales tactics.

Think about a friend recommending a restaurant.
Friend A says:
“You HAVE to go there. Best restaurant in the city. Best food. Best service. Best everything. You will absolutely love it.”
Friend B says:
“I've been there twice. The food is genuinely good. But don't order the pasta. It's average. Get the grilled chicken.”
Who do you trust more?
Probably Friend B.
Because the recommendation contains friction.
It's not trying to make everything sound perfect.
And that's exactly what makes it believable.

That's why I think honesty isn't simply telling the truth.
It's also being willing to tell the uncomfortable truth.
Anyone can tell the customer:
“You're making a great decision.”
The real test is whether you can say:
“I don't think this is the right decision for you.”
Anyone can say:
“Our product is perfect for you.”
The real test is whether you can say:
“Actually, based on what you've told me, I think another option may suit you better.”
Anyone can say:
“We can definitely do it.”
The real test is whether you can say:
“I don't know yet. Let me find out.”
That's where trust gets built.

And this is why I think trust is not built during the big presentation.
It's built in tiny moments.
You said you'd call at 5.
Did you call at 5?
You said you'd send the proposal today.
Did you send it?
You promised to check something.
Did you check it?
You said implementation would take 10 days.
Did it actually take 10 days?
These seem small.
They're not.
Because the buyer is constantly collecting evidence.
Every small interaction answers the same silent question:
“Can I rely on this person?”
That's why trust can take months to build...
and five minutes to damage.

There's one more thing I want to separate.
Confidence is not trust.
A salesperson can walk into a room with unbelievable confidence.
Strong voice.
Perfect posture.
No hesitation.
Great presentation.
And still make you nervous.
Why?
Because confidence tells me:
“You believe in yourself.”
Trust tells me:
“I believe in you.”
Those are completely different things.
And I think great salespeople learn to stop chasing the first...
and start earning the second.

So what actually makes someone trustworthy?
I don't think it's one magical technique.
It's a pattern.
Competence
“This person knows what they're doing.”
Honesty
“This person will tell me the truth.”
Judgment
“This person understands my situation.”
Consistency
“This person does what they say.”
Intent
“This person isn't just trying to get my money.”
And when those signals keep showing up...
the buyer reaches a conclusion.
Not because you told them to.
Not because you asked them to.
But because you gave them enough evidence.
“I trust this person's judgment.”
That's the shift.

And maybe that's why I don't believe in “building trust” as a performance.
The moment you start thinking:
“How do I make this buyer trust me?”
you can become artificial.
You start acting trustworthy.
Saying the right things.
Using the right body language.
Trying to look confident.
Trying to look honest.
Trying to look like an expert.
And ironically...
the buyer can feel that you're trying.
Instead, ask a different question:
“What would I do if I genuinely cared about helping this person make the right decision?”
That question is much harder.
Because sometimes the answer is:
Say no.
Sometimes:
Admit you don't know.
Sometimes:
Challenge them.
Sometimes:
Recommend a cheaper option.
Sometimes:
Tell them to wait.
And sometimes...
walk away.

That's also why I don't believe you are in the business of making people like you.
You are in the business of helping them make the right decision.
If they like you...
great.
If they don't think you're particularly funny...
you'll survive. 😂
If they don't find you charming...
that's okay.
But if they believe:
“This person understands my situation.”
“This person isn't hiding anything.”
“This person will tell me if I'm wrong.”
“This person cares about the outcome.”
Then you've built something much more valuable than rapport.
You've built trust.

So the next time you're sitting across from a buyer...
don't ask:
“How can I make them like me?”
Don't even ask:
“How can I prove I'm an expert?”
Ask yourself something harder:
“If I were sitting in their chair, would I trust my own recommendation?”
If the answer is no...
don't work harder on your pitch.
Work harder on your judgment.
Because the buyer doesn't need you to be the most charming person in the room.
They don't need you to have the biggest client list.
They don't need you to have an answer for everything.
They need enough evidence to reach one conclusion:
“I trust this person's judgment.”
And once you've earned that...
you don't need to keep selling trust.
You've already earned the right to be heard.

Article-3
Why The Best Salespeople Sometimes Talk Less
Sales // Conversation Dynamics™
I've probably heard more sales advice than most salespeople should ever hear. 😂
And the strange thing is...
a lot of it works.
Until you put it in the wrong situation.
That's where things get interesting.
Because somewhere along the way, sales training started turning useful techniques into universal laws.
“Always do this.”
“Never say that.”
“Always ask open-ended questions.”
“Never discuss price first.”
“Always build rapport before talking business.”
“Always overcome objections.”
“Never let the customer leave without closing.”
And after hearing enough of these...
you start believing there's a secret operating system for selling.
Learn the framework.
Memorize the script.
Apply it.
Close the deal.
Simple.
Except...
real buyers didn't get the memo. 😂

I learned this while working in sales.
I don't remember the exact date, but it was during one of the phases of my career when I was working closely with a sales team.
I remember our manager conducting training for the team.
The environment was pretty typical.
A group of salespeople sitting together, notebooks open, everyone listening.
Our manager was explaining a particular sales framework.
The idea was simple:
When the customer says X, do Y.
It sounded logical.
Actually...
it sounded very convincing.
The framework had steps.
Questions.
Responses.
A sequence.
And the way it was being explained, it almost sounded like:
“If you follow this properly, the customer will move forward.”
And I remember thinking:
Okay. Let's try it.
So the team started using it.
And that's when I noticed something strange.
It worked beautifully...
in some conversations.
But in others?
It felt completely unnatural.
Sometimes the customer didn't respond the way the framework expected.
Sometimes the buyer asked something completely different.
Sometimes the industry itself had a different buying process.
Sometimes the decision-maker wasn't even in the conversation.
And sometimes...
the framework was technically being followed, but the conversation was getting worse.
I remember feeling frustrated.
Then irritated.
Because I kept thinking:
“If this is supposed to work, why isn't it working here?”
And then another thought came:
“Maybe the problem isn't the salesperson.”
Maybe...
the framework was being treated like a law when it was actually just a tool.
That distinction changed the way I looked at sales training.

And I don't blame my manager.
That's important.
I don't think he was deliberately teaching the team something wrong.
He was teaching what he knew.
The bigger problem was something I've seen in sales training again and again:
People sometimes confuse what worked for them with what works everywhere.
If you've sold one type of product...
to one type of buyer...
in one type of industry...
with one kind of sales cycle...
you can easily start believing:
“This is how sales works.”
But maybe...
that's just how your sales environment worked.
And there's a huge difference.

Think about this.
Would you use exactly the same sales conversation for:
A ₹2,000 consumer product?
A ₹2 lakh service?
A ₹20 lakh enterprise solution?
A ₹50 lakh industrial machine?
A consulting engagement?
A real-estate purchase?
A software subscription?
A business acquisition?
Of course not.
Because the buyer changes.
The risk changes.
The decision-making changes.
The sales cycle changes.
The number of decision-makers changes.
The consequences of making the wrong decision change.
So why would the exact same technique work identically in all of them?
It shouldn't.
And yet...
that's exactly how some sales training is taught.

This is where I want to break one of the biggest sales beliefs.
A framework is not a law.
A framework is a way of thinking.
A structure.
A map.
A tool.
It can be incredibly useful.
But it doesn't replace judgment.
Think about GPS.
You enter the destination.
It gives you a route.
Perfect.
But imagine the road is blocked.
The GPS says:
“Turn left.”
You look ahead.
There's a huge construction barrier.
Do you say:
“But the framework says left.” 😂
No.
You adapt.
You find another route.
The map is useful.
But you still need someone in the driver's seat.
That's how I see sales frameworks.
Framework = map.
Judgment = knowing when the map doesn't match the road.
And real sales conversations are full of roads the map didn't predict.

This is also why I get uncomfortable when someone says:
“This technique works every time.”
Every time?
For whom?
In which industry?
At what price?
With what buyer?
At what stage?
With what level of trust?
Under what circumstances?
The moment someone says:
“Always...”
or
“Never...”
my next question is:
“In what situation?”
Because sales is contextual.
And context changes everything.

Here's another belief I want to challenge.
“The more you talk, the more you sell.”
It sounds logical.
You're explaining the product.
You're answering questions.
You're presenting benefits.
You're handling objections.
You're giving examples.
You're proving value.
You're doing everything a salesperson is supposed to do.
Except...
sometimes you're doing too much.
I've made this mistake myself.
Especially when I was starting out.
A buyer would become quiet.
And instead of becoming curious...
I'd become nervous.
I'd think:
“Why aren't they responding?”
So I'd explain more.
Another benefit.
Another example.
Another proof point.
Another reason.
And internally, I felt productive.
But sometimes...
I wasn't helping the buyer.
I was calming myself down.
That's a very uncomfortable realization.
Sometimes salespeople keep talking because silence makes them uncomfortable.
Not because the buyer needs more information.

Think about what happens when someone says:
“Hmm... I need to think about this.”
The old-school salesperson often thinks:
“Objection! Handle it.”
So they immediately jump in.
“Absolutely, but let me explain one more thing...”
Then another thing.
Then another.
The buyer is trying to process the decision.
The salesperson is trying to stop the buyer from processing the decision. 😂
Sometimes the best response is simply:
“Of course. What part would you like to think about?”
Now you're learning.
You're not fighting.

And this brings me to another sales belief:
“Always overcome objections.”
I don't agree.
Some objections should be explored.
Some should be answered.
Some should be challenged.
Some aren't objections at all.
And some...
should simply be respected.
Imagine a buyer says:
“The timing isn't right. We're going through a major internal change.”
Do you immediately launch your objection-handling framework?
Maybe.
Or maybe...
you say:
“That makes sense. What would need to change internally before this becomes a priority?”
Now you're having a conversation.
Not performing a technique.
That's a major difference.

Here's another one.
“Always ask open-ended questions.”
It's good advice.
Sometimes.
But not always.
Imagine you ask:
“Tell me more about your sales process.”
That's open-ended.
Good question.
But sometimes a more specific question gives you much better information:
“How many people are currently responsible for following up with leads?”
Or:
“How long does it usually take your team to contact a new lead?”
Or:
“Who actually makes the final decision?”
The question isn't powerful because it's open-ended.
It's powerful because it reveals something useful.
That's a very different way of thinking.

Same thing with “always build rapport first.”
I've seen salespeople spend ten minutes discussing:
Cricket.
Travel.
Weather.
Family.
The city.
Weekend plans.
Because somebody told them:
“Rapport must come before business.”
😂
But what if the buyer has already blocked 30 minutes and wants to get straight to the problem?
Now your “rapport” is becoming friction.
The buyer might be thinking:
“Can we just get to the point?”
Rapport is useful.
But forced rapport is not rapport.
Context decides.

Same with price.
“Never discuss price too early.”
Good principle in some situations.
Bad rule in others.
If someone has a budget of ₹2 lakh...
and you're selling a ₹25 lakh solution...
why waste an hour pretending price doesn't matter?
Sometimes bringing the investment into the conversation early actually saves everyone's time.
Again...
I'm not saying:
“Always discuss price early.”
I'm saying:
“Stop looking for absolute rules where context matters.”

And this is where I think sales trainers can unintentionally create weak salespeople.
Not because frameworks are bad.
Because framework dependency is bad.
A salesperson learns:
“If the buyer says this, I say that.”
So they memorize.
Buyer says something unexpected.
Salesperson freezes.
Why?
Because the situation wasn't in the script.
That's the problem.
The salesperson learned responses.
They didn't learn judgment.

Think about two salespeople.
Salesperson A:
Has memorized 30 objection-handling responses.
Buyer gives objection #31.
Silence.
Salesperson B:
Has fewer scripts.
But understands:
buyer psychology
business context
decision-making
value
risk
urgency
questions
human behavior
Buyer gives objection #31.
Salesperson B thinks:
“Interesting. What's actually happening here?”
That's the person I want.
Because sales doesn't happen inside a training manual.
It happens in reality.
And reality is wonderfully bad at following scripts. 😂

Now, before someone misunderstands me...
I absolutely believe in sales frameworks.
In fact, I use them.
Some frameworks are extremely powerful.
Some principles work across industries.
Some questioning structures work almost everywhere.
Some psychological principles are deeply rooted in human behavior.
But here's my belief:
The truly universal sales principles are rare. Very rare.
And the reason they survive across industries is usually because they are based on human behavior, not a particular sales environment.
For example:
Understand before prescribing.
Don't lie.
Don't promise what you can't deliver.
Ask questions that improve your understanding.
Create value before asking for commitment.
Don't confuse pressure with persuasion.
These aren't tricks.
They're principles.
And principles are much more portable than techniques.

That's the distinction I wish more salespeople understood.
Technique asks:
“What should I say?”
Framework asks:
“What should I look for?”
Judgment asks:
“What's actually happening here?”
And that third question...
is where the real game begins.

Because sometimes the best sales move is to talk.
Sometimes it's to ask.
Sometimes it's to challenge.
Sometimes it's to explain.
Sometimes it's to stay silent.
Sometimes it's to follow up tomorrow.
Sometimes it's to walk away.
Sometimes it's to say:
“I don't think we're the right fit.”
And sometimes...
it's to say absolutely nothing.
The salesperson who knows only techniques has a problem:
They need the situation to look familiar.
The salesperson with judgment can handle unfamiliar situations.
That's the difference.

And here's why the best salespeople sometimes talk less.
It's not because they have discovered some magical “talk less, close more” technique.
It's because they don't panic when they don't know what to say.
They observe.
They listen.
They diagnose.
They think.
Then they speak.
And because they have actually understood what's happening...
they often need fewer words.
A beginner thinks:
“What should I say next?”
An experienced salesperson thinks:
“What is actually happening right now?”
That's a completely different mental state.

I remember seeing the opposite too.
A salesperson would finish asking a question...
the buyer would pause for three seconds...
and the salesperson would immediately jump in.
Three seconds.
That's all.
😂
They couldn't tolerate the silence.
So they started talking.
And sometimes that extra sentence...
created the very doubt that didn't exist before.
That's one of the strangest things about selling.
You can talk yourself out of a deal that was already moving toward you.
The buyer was thinking:
“Yes, this makes sense.”
And your brain said:
“Let's add another 90 seconds of explanation.”
Why?
Because you didn't trust the silence.

The best salespeople understand something simple:
Silence is not always a problem that needs to be fixed.
Sometimes the buyer is thinking.
Let them think.
Sometimes they're processing.
Let them process.
Sometimes they're deciding.
Don't interrupt the decision.
And sometimes...
they're waiting for you to stop talking. 😂
Knowing which one it is...
that's judgment.

This is also why I don't want salespeople who can only sell in familiar situations.
I want someone who can walk into:
A new industry.
A new product.
A new buyer.
A new sales cycle.
A new objection.
A completely unfamiliar conversation...
and still think:
“Okay. I don't have the answer yet. But I can figure out what's happening.”
That's a much more valuable skill than memorizing another 20 closing techniques.
Because techniques expire.
Markets change.
Buyer behavior changes.
Industries change.
Technology changes.
But the ability to think clearly inside a conversation...
stays valuable.

And this is actually one of the reasons I built Authority Closers the way I did.
I don't want to create salespeople who collect techniques.
I don't want someone to leave training with:
“I know 17 objection-handling methods.”
Great.
What happens when objection #18 walks through the door?
I want them to have something deeper.
I want them to be able to look at a situation and think:
“What's really happening here?”
Then decide what to do.
That's what I call becoming an Authority Closer.
Not someone who has a script for everything.
Someone who can think when the script stops working.

So here's my challenge to you.
The next time someone teaches you a sales rule, don't blindly accept it.
Ask:
“When does this work?”
Then ask:
“When doesn't it work?”
Then:
“Why?”
And if the trainer can't answer the third question...
be careful.
Because memorizing a technique is easy.
Understanding its boundaries is where expertise begins.

Here's a simple test I use now.
When someone says:
“Always do this.”
Ask:
“In which situation?”
When someone says:
“Never do this.”
Ask:
“Why?”
When someone says:
“This works every time.”
Ask:
“For which buyer?”
Those three questions can save you from a lot of bad sales advice.

Because the biggest danger isn't learning a bad technique.
It's learning a good technique and using it at the wrong time.
That's much harder to detect.
Because the technique actually works.
Just...
not here.
And when it fails, you blame yourself.
“Maybe I'm not good at sales.”
Maybe.
Or maybe...
you were given a hammer and told every sales problem was a nail.

That's why I believe great sales training shouldn't create technique collectors.
It should create decision-makers.
People who know when to speak.
When to ask.
When to challenge.
When to listen.
When to follow up.
When to stop.
When to push.
When to step back.
When to use a framework.
And when to throw the framework away.
Because that's what real selling looks like.
Not:
“Step 1. Step 2. Step 3. Close.”
But:
“Here's the situation. What's actually happening?”
Then you decide.

A script can tell you what to say.
A framework can tell you what to look for.
But judgment tells you what to do when reality doesn't follow the framework.
And reality...
never follows the framework perfectly.
That's why the best salespeople sometimes talk less.
They're not sitting there thinking:
“What should I say next?”
They're thinking:
“What is actually happening?”
And once you understand that...
you don't need to talk all the time.
Sometimes...
the smartest thing a salesperson can say is nothing.
